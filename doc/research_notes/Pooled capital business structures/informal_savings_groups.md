# Informal and Semi-Formal Pooled Savings, Credit and Investment Groups (ROSCAs, ASCAs, thrift co-ops, investment clubs, chamas, chit funds, and their digitisation)

Scope note: Nigeria first, then Kenya, Ghana, India and other comparators. Figures carry their year. A recurring theme across every model below is the same tension: the models work because of social collateral (trust, reputation, repeated interaction), and they fail at precisely the point where that collateral is stretched — a stranger-collector, a group that has grown too big to monitor, or a "club" that is really a pyramid.

---

## Key Question 1: ROSCA mechanics (ajo / esusu / adashe) — contributions, rotation order, collector fee, cycle, default, and implicit interest

### Takeaway
A ROSCA is a fixed-contribution, fixed-membership pool handed in full to one member per round; rotation order (ballot, consensus/need, auction, or organiser-first) is the whole economics of the thing — early receivers are borrowers, late receivers are lenders, and in bidding variants the auction discount is the implicit interest. In Nigeria's urban ajo variant a professional collector (alajo) typically keeps one round's contribution as commission; enforcement is social (oath, reputation, exclusion), not legal.

### Cited Findings

**Definition and basic paradigm**
- Shirley Ardener's 1964 definition: a ROSCA is "an association formed upon a core of participants who agree to make regular contributions to a fund which is given, in whole or in part, to each contributor in rotation." — [Federal Reserve Bank of Philadelphia discussion paper on ROSCAs, citing Ardener 1964, p. 201](https://www.philadelphiafed.org/-/media/frbp/assets/community-development/discussion-papers/discussionpaper-ROSCAs.pdf)
- Standard paradigm: "A group of people assemble, usually at the behest of a leader or organizer"; e.g. an organizer needing $1,000 gathers nine people he believes credible, each (including himself) contributes $100 per meeting, and the organizer takes the first pot. "Normally, the organizer, in exchange for undertaking the administrative duties of organizing the ROSCA," receives the pot first. — [Philadelphia Fed](https://www.philadelphiafed.org/-/media/frbp/assets/community-development/discussion-papers/discussionpaper-ROSCAs.pdf)
- Besley, Coate and Loury (1993) classify ROSCAs that allocate by lottery as "random ROSCAs" and those that allocate by auction as "bidding ROSCAs"; in their model (individuals saving for an indivisible durable good) both types improve welfare over autarky, and random allocation is preferred when members have identical tastes, though not necessarily under heterogeneity. — [Besley, Coate & Loury, AER 1993 (ISI mirror)](http://www.isid.ac.in/~tridip/Teaching/DevelopmentMicroeconomics/Readings/Besley&Coate&Loury-AER1993.pdf); summarised in [ResearchGate: The Economics of ROSCAs](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/40624313_The_Economics_of_Rotating_Savings_and_Credit_Associations)
- Choice between random and bidding allocation analysed in Kovsted & Lyk-Jensen (J. Dev. Econ. 1999). — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/deveco/v60y1999i1p143-172.html)

**Nigeria: esusu / ajo / adashe**
- Regional names: esusu/esu (Yoruba), etoto (Igbo/Ibibio), adashi (Hausa), dashi (Nupe), osusu (Ogoja), isusu (other Igbo areas), asun (Ishan), bam (Tiv); cognates include tontine (Cameroon/Niger), susu (Ghana), "partner" (Jamaica). First documented academically by Bascom (1952) among the Yoruba. — [Global Informality Project: Esusu (Nigeria)](https://www.in-formality.com/wiki/index.php?title=Esusu_%28Nigeria%29)
- Contribution schedule: members contribute "a fixed and equal sum of money at specific intervals – daily, weekly, fortnightly, monthly or bi-monthly"; when everyone has received the pool once "a new rotation cycle is launched." — [Global Informality Project](https://www.in-formality.com/wiki/index.php?title=Esusu_%28Nigeria%29)
- Rotation order: "usually decided by means of a ballot or by consensus"; groups may skip the lottery and agree consensually when each member receives, reverting to drawing tickets if too many want the same month (i.e. need-based with lottery as tie-break). — [Global Informality Project](https://www.in-formality.com/wiki/index.php?title=Esusu_%28Nigeria%29)
- Collector variant (urban ajo): a professional collector, the alajo, goes round collecting daily/weekly and "is paid a small commission for this service," typically "one of the rounds of the total contribution"; the alajo may deposit the funds in a bank, acting as a bridge to the formal system. — [Global Informality Project](https://www.in-formality.com/wiki/index.php?title=Esusu_%28Nigeria%29)
- Default/enforcement: the system "tends to function solely on an oath of allegiance and mutual trust"; members take an oath to stop early receivers withdrawing; for ajo, "the credibility of the alajo plays the key role in preventing risk." No formal penalty schedule is documented. — [Global Informality Project](https://www.in-formality.com/wiki/index.php?title=Esusu_%28Nigeria%29)
- Lagos market survey (N=1,200 traders across six markets, field survey 2024): contribution frequency weekly 51.7%, monthly 24.9%, daily 14.8%, irregular 8.6%; primary motive access to credit 43.5%, savings 39.0%, social support 17.5%; 70.6% had participated 1–5 years. — [Adeyemi et al., African Scholars Multidisciplinary Journal, Jan 2025](https://www.gojamss.net/journal/index.php/ASMJ/article/viewFile/1285/1297)
- An Ogun State study reports a 43.6% ROSCA participation rate in its sample, with education, marital status, sex and income as significant predictors; participation is higher among women. — [Determinants of Participation in ROSCAs in Ogun State (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362900529_DETERMINANTS_OF_PARTICIPATION_IN_ROTATING_SAVINGS_AND_CREDIT_ASSOCIATIONS_ROSCAs_IN_OGUN_STATE_NIGERIA)
- Ajo described (Dec 2024) as participants making "daily or weekly cash contributions to a money holder" and receiving "a periodic lump sum"; "millions of Nigerians" rely on it; the government "does not have any role directly to protect informal savings schemes" (development economist Hauwa Mustapha). — [VOA News, 6 Dec 2024](https://www.voanews.com/a/nigerians-turn-to-community-savings-amid-financial-struggles-/7890615.html)

**Implicit interest: early vs late receivers**
- In a random ROSCA, "the organizer who obtains the pot in the first round is effectively receiving an interest-free loan"; all other participants "begin the ROSCA as lenders, not borrowers" and the cost of lending is forgone interest. — [Philadelphia Fed](https://www.philadelphiafed.org/-/media/frbp/assets/community-development/discussion-papers/discussionpaper-ROSCAs.pdf)
- In a bidding ROSCA the highest bidder takes the pot less the winning bid, and the bid is distributed to the others as a dividend; higher winning bids therefore mean higher payouts to later recipients. — [Klonner & Rai, "Adverse Selection in Credit Markets: Evidence from South Indian Bidding Roscas" (2005)](https://eml.berkeley.edu/~webfac/emiguel/e271_s06/klonner.pdf)
- Worked toy example (3 members, $10/month, winning bids $15, $12, $0): first recipient nets $10 and repays $6 and $10 — "a 34 percentage monthly interest rate" as borrower; last recipient saves $5, $6 and receives $20 — "a monthly interest rate of 49 percent" as saver. — [Klonner & Rai 2005](https://eml.berkeley.edu/~webfac/emiguel/e271_s06/klonner.pdf)
- Real data: a 40-member bidding ROSCA in an Asian community in Philadelphia (started Oct 2003, $1,000/month contributions, $40,000 pot) produced ex-post annual borrowing rates for rounds 2–7 of 2.74%–7.37% ("upfront fees" method) or 2.81%–7.44% (IRR method), versus 3-month CDs near 1.0% and prime C&I loan rates of 4.36% at the time; the organizer (P1) took the full $40,000 at 0%. — [Philadelphia Fed](https://www.philadelphiafed.org/-/media/frbp/assets/community-development/discussion-papers/discussionpaper-ROSCAs.pdf)

**Default evidence (commercially organised bidding ROSCAs)**
- Shriram Chits (Tamil Nadu; 78 branches in 1991, 87 by May 2005): the organiser takes the first pot at zero bid and "a commission (usually 5–6 percent) of the pot in each round"; recipients in the first half of long-duration groups must provide three cosigners with combined monthly income of 15% of the pot, second-half recipients two; the company admits only those with "a regular wage"; home-visit collection. — [Klonner & Rai 2005](https://eml.berkeley.edu/~webfac/emiguel/e271_s06/klonner.pdf)
- Average total default rate 1.6% (excluding institutional members, who were 10–25% of members), compared with 4.5% US credit-card charge-offs. Individual default rates before the 1993 bid ceiling: 1.74% for first-half (early) recipients vs 0.54% for second-half recipients; after the ceiling 1.53% vs 0.73%; total default fell 18% from 1.71% to 1.41%. The authors read this as adverse selection — borrowers willing to pay the highest implicit interest are the riskiest. — [Klonner & Rai 2005](https://eml.berkeley.edu/~webfac/emiguel/e271_s06/klonner.pdf)

### Inferences
- Nigerian ajo/esusu mostly uses ballot or need/consensus rather than auction, so implicit interest is redistributive by luck or need rather than priced; the "price" is instead paid uniformly as the alajo's one-round commission (e.g. 1/31 ≈ 3.2% of a daily 31-day cycle, or 1/12 ≈ 8.3% of a 12-round monthly cycle — a very high effective annual cost for what is a zero-interest deposit).
- The Klonner–Rai finding (early receivers default 2–3× more than late receivers) is the quantitative core of the ROSCA failure mode: the people who want the pot first are, on average, the people least able to keep paying — which is why strong groups insist on guarantors for early positions, and why organiser-first is a standard compensation design.
- The Philadelphia data show bidding ROSCA borrowing costs cluster between bank CD rates and prime loan rates, i.e. ROSCAs can be competitive credit for members shut out of banks.

### Gaps
- No Nigerian survey found that quantifies ROSCA default rates or collector-absconding frequency with a representative sample; the Lagos market study gives perceptions (see Q6), not loss rates.
- I found no primary source confirming the exact split of Nigerian groups by rotation rule (ballot vs need vs seniority vs auction).
- The Philadelphia Fed summary produced by the fetch tool included "participation 20–80%" and "default 5–15% (Ardener/Bouman)" figures that I could not locate in the paper text; I have excluded them as unverified.

## Key Question 2: ASCA / accumulating models and cooperative thrift & credit — saving, lending at interest, share-out, record-keeping

### Takeaway
ASCAs keep the pot rather than rotating it, lend it to members at interest (commonly 5–20% per month in VSLA-type groups; 1–2% per month in formal Nigerian thrift co-ops), and at year-end divide the whole fund — principal plus interest and fines — pro rata to each member's shares; the share-out arithmetic (fund ÷ total shares = new share value) is what makes variable savings fair, and a locked cash box, passbooks and a multi-officer committee are the record-keeping backbone.

### Cited Findings

**VSLA / savings-group (ASCA) methodology — the global reference design**
- Groups of 15–25 members; members buy shares at a group-set price, 1–5 shares per meeting; loans up to three times a member's savings for up to three months; interest commonly 10% (groups set 5–20%); a separate social fund (equal contributions of ¼–½ of a share) for emergencies, interest-free; cash kept in "a cash box…secured with three padlocks, with three group members each holding one of the keys"; five-member committee (chair, record-keeper, box-keeper, two money counters) elected at least annually; cycles "of generally about one year" ending in share-out. — [CARE VSLA 101](https://www.care.org/our-work/economic-growth/savings-groups/vsla-101/)
- Share-out calculation: record-keeper totals all shares saved by the group; money counters count all cash (excluding social fund); total money ÷ total shares = new share value; each member receives new share value × shares in their passbook. The share-out therefore distributes all profit from interest and fines in proportion to savings. — [VSLA.net methodology](https://www.vsla.net/the-vsla-methodology/)
- Default handling: cancelling a defaulting member's shares penalises them because they lose the profit on those shares; the procedure must be defined in the group constitution. — [VSLA.net methodology](https://www.vsla.net/the-vsla-methodology/)
- Each group sets its own share price, loan interest, loan durations, social-fund contribution and fines schedule. — [VSLA.net](https://www.vsla.net/the-vsla-methodology/)

**Evidence on VSLA outcomes (IPA randomised evaluation)**
- RCT across 561 village clusters / 15,221 households in Ghana, Malawi and Uganda, 2008–2011: take-up averaged 32%; 61% of members completed at least one cycle; total savings rose US$14 (PPP 2011), +34% vs comparison; 68% of members took at least one loan, median interest 10% with 1–3 month terms; no detectable impact on average consumption; +6% household businesses, +9% business duration; +4 pp women's control over business decisions and food spending; programme cost $7.01 (Malawi) to $10.85 (Uganda) per household. — [IPA: Impact of Savings Groups on the Lives of the Rural Poor](https://poverty-action.org/study/impact-savings-groups-lives-rural-poor-ghana-malawi-and-uganda); cluster RCT also in [Ksoll et al., J. Dev. Econ. 2016](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0304387815001352)

**Kenyan "table banking" (ASCA variant)**
- 10–30 members meet monthly; savings, repayments and interest are placed "on the table" and immediately re-lent to members at an agreed rate, with short-term and long-term loans; "character is the collateral." — [Africa.com on table banking](https://africa.com/table-banking-helping-women-in-kenya-to-put-food-on-the-table/); [re-solve global health](https://www.re-solveglobalhealth.com/post/table-banking-kenyan-women-bank-on-self-sufficiency-across-a-table)
- Table-banking interest in Kenyan chamas typically 5–10% per month, with interest earnings growing the fund. — [Huduma Global: Understanding Chamas in Kenya](https://hudumaglobal.com/blog/understanding-chamas-kenya-investment-groups-merry-go-rounds-collective-saving)
- Joyful Women Organisation (JoyWo): founded 2009 with 60 women in Eldoret; now reports presence in 44 counties, "over 240,000 members revolving KES 3.4 billion." — [JoyWo](https://joywo.org/); [Kenyans.co.ke](https://www.kenyans.co.ke/news/118434-what-you-should-know-about-rachel-rutos-41billion-joyful-women-initiative)

**Nigerian cooperative thrift & credit societies (registered ASCAs)**
- Core function: financial intermediation — making it easier for low-income people to save so more is available to lend to members; loans on easier terms than banks; interest income and investment returns "may either be distributed to the members or reinvested in the society"; members also earn interest on savings. — [The Conversation: How thrift and credit societies can help Africa](https://theconversation.com/how-thrift-and-credit-societies-can-help-africa-promote-entrepreneurship-50781); [Credit and thrift co-operatives in Nigeria (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/262398845_Credit_and_thrift_co-operatives_in_Nigeria_A_potential_source_of_capital_formation_and_employment)
- Reported practice: loan rates "as low as 1% monthly" with fellow members as guarantors; mandatory monthly contributions commonly ₦2,000–₦10,000; share capital ₦10,000–₦100,000; dividends "typically paid annually, ranging from 5–15% of savings"; one society charged members 6% and non-members 10%. — [KudiCompass guide (2026; secondary/how-to source)](https://kudicompass.com/how-to-join-cooperative-society-nigeria/)
- Registration: application to the state Director of Cooperatives under the Nigerian Cooperative Societies Act (Lagos: Ministry of Commerce, Industry and Cooperatives); minimum ten signatories; bye-laws filed; no member may hold more than 20% of shares; societies "may invest their funds in a bank, in federal government-backed securities, or in any other manner provided for in their constitution"; exempt from company income tax on their activities and from CAMA, but must file returns to the Director. — [FurtherAfrica, Nov 2020](https://furtherafrica.com/2020/11/19/establishing-a-cooperative-society-for-investment-purposes-in-nigeria/)
- EFInA 2023 lists co-operatives, savings groups, savings thrift and village associations as the main informal/semi-formal providers, with "significant increase in the use of village associations predominantly in the South East." — [EFInA A2F 2023 launch presentation](https://efina.org.ng/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/A2F-2023-Event-Day-Presentation-Version4-1.pdf)

### Inferences
- The VSLA share-out formula is the informal-sector equivalent of a unit/NAV system (Q3): because the share value is recomputed at the end from the actual cash, members who saved more earn more, and interest is implicitly credited at the same rate per share regardless of when saved within the cycle.
- At 5–10% per month, table-banking interest paid by borrowing members is far above formal rates, but because borrowers and savers are the same people, much of it is a transfer back to themselves at share-out; the IPA RCT's "no consumption impact" result suggests the welfare gain is in smoothing and business formation, not returns.
- Registered Nigerian co-ops reporting 5–15% annual dividends are in the same range as, or below, Nigerian T-bill yields in 2023–2025, so their appeal is credit access and discipline rather than yield.

### Gaps
- No Nigeria-specific dataset on cooperative loan default rates or dividend distributions was found; the KudiCompass figures are from a how-to blog, not a regulator or survey.
- The widely repeated "4–5% per month ≈ 60% a year return on savings" claim for VSLAs surfaced in search summaries but I could not tie it to a primary document; treat as unverified. The ResearchGate paper "Small groups, large profits: calculating interest rates in community-managed microfinance" (likely the origin) returned HTTP 403.

## Key Question 3: Investment clubs — pooling to buy stocks, T-bills, property or fund a business; unit (NAV) valuation; typical rules

### Takeaway
Investment clubs solve the "joined-at-different-times" problem with unit valuation: net assets ÷ units outstanding gives a unit price on each valuation date, new money buys units at that price and withdrawals redeem at it. Nigerian clubs typically organise as cooperatives (tax-exempt, ≥10 members), partnerships or companies; SEC registration is generally not required for small private clubs but becomes an issue when a club solicits the public or grows large.

### Cited Findings

**Unit valuation (NAV per unit)**
- "To find the current unit value, divide the Net Worth of the Club by the total units." A member's contribution ÷ unit value = units bought. Example: Member A $100 and Member B $200 at unit value 10 → 10 and 20 units; portfolio rises to $400 → unit value $400 ÷ 30 = $13.33; next month A adds $200 (15 units), B adds $100 (7.5 units). Unit systems are "fair, and allow members to start out giving very small amounts, even $20"; equal-share systems force new members to buy in at the club's full per-share value. — [ICLUBcentral: What is the Unit Valuation System?](https://www.iclub.com/clubs/unit_value.asp)
- Payments never change the unit value (they add equal amounts to net worth and units); clubs should fix a valuation date in their bylaws (e.g. monthly meeting date). — [myICLUB club accounting](https://www.myiclub.com/clubhub/clubaccounting.aspx)
- UK club example (CAMRA Members' Investment Club): units issued and redeemed at NAV on the last business day of the month, subject to a bid/offer spread; a joining member's subscription buys units at the prevailing unit value; leavers are repaid at the net valuation of the last business day of the month. — [CMIC Club Rules](https://cmic.uk.com/rules/)
- Typical rule set (model constitution): 3–20 members; regular contributions by standing order into a club bank account; majority voting; defined notice for withdrawal. — [TimeToTrade model Investment Club Rules](https://wiki.timetotrade.com/Investment_Club_Rules); [Combined constitution](https://wiki.timetotrade.com/Investment_Club_Constitution_&_Rules_(Combined))

**Nigeria-specific**
- Legal forms: partnership, limited liability company, or cooperative society (the latter "may provide access to government-backed loans and grants," especially for agriculture). "Most small investment clubs do not need to register with the Nigerian Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC), [but] clubs with larger pools of assets or those open to the public might be subject to SEC regulations." Recommended rules: membership fee/minimum investment plus regular dues; majority vote on investments; monthly or quarterly meetings; written member agreement covering contributions, voting rights, profit distribution and dispute resolution; elected president/treasurer/secretary; dedicated bank account; CAC annual returns and FIRS compliance where incorporated. — [Lexpraxis Solicitors: How to start an investment club in Nigeria](https://lexpraxisng.com/how-to-start-an-investment-club-in-nigeria/)
- Cooperative route: ≥10 members, bye-laws, 20% individual shareholding cap, may invest in banks and FGN securities, exempt from company income tax. — [FurtherAfrica 2020](https://furtherafrica.com/2020/11/19/establishing-a-cooperative-society-for-investment-purposes-in-nigeria/)
- Pooling into T-bills: CBN primary-market minimum is ₦50 million, but banks pool customer money from as little as ₦100,000 and share interest pro rata (e.g. ₦100,000 in a 364-day bill at 10% earns ₦10,000 gross). — [Cowrywise blog: Treasury Bills in Nigeria](https://cowrywise.com/blog/treasury-bills-in-nigeria/)
- SEC Nigeria defines and regulates Collective Investment Schemes; ISA 2025 (Sections 150–151) for the first time brings PE and VC funds inside the CIS framework. — [UUBO FAQ on ISA 2025](https://uubo.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/UUBO-FAQs-for-Private-Capital-Players-Nigerias-Investments-and-Securities-Act-2025-UUBO-ADM-0233-1.pdf); [SEC training deck on CIS](https://home.sec.gov.ng/documents/1121/SEC-Training-Collective-Investment-Schemes-Achieving-Diversity-in-Investments-fnl.pdf)

**Kenya comparator (investment chamas)**
- Share-based chamas: members buy shares representing ownership percentages; leadership invests in agreed ventures — "loans to members at 20% per month," transport, real estate, agriculture, financial instruments. — [Wikipedia: Chama (investment)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chama_(investment))
- Safaricom Investment Co-operative, set up 2009 under the Co-operatives Act for joint member investment, has been in real estate since inception (Zaria Village, Rongai Royal Villas, Blue Bells Gardens, Ruaka Ridge) and in 2023 was building 200 affordable units in Ruaka; it planned to diversify into health, education and agribusiness. — [Capital FM, Apr 2023](https://capitalfm.co.ke/business/2023/04/safaricom-investment-co-operative-leads-kenyas-affordable-housing-projects); [Nation](https://nation.africa/kenya/business/safaricom-sacco-mulls-investments-beyond-real-estate-92392)

### Inferences
- The cooperative form is the natural Nigerian wrapper for an investment club: it gives legal personality to hold assets, tax exemption, and state-registrar oversight, while the 20% shareholding cap and one-member-one-vote norms prevent capture — but it also puts the club outside SEC's investor-protection perimeter, which is exactly the gap "Famzhi Interbiz" exploited by "posing as a cooperative" (see Q6).
- A club that fixes a valuation date, prices units at NAV, and requires written withdrawal notice (one to three months is the common range in model rules) is structurally a private open-ended fund; once it markets units to non-members it becomes a CIS and needs SEC registration.

### Gaps
- I found no published Nigerian survey on the number of investment clubs, their median size, or realised returns; coverage is how-to/legal commentary. The TechCabal piece on YC-backed "Rank" (Sept 2026) that might carry market figures returned HTTP 403.
- No Nigerian source gave a standard withdrawal-notice period; the 1–3 month figure above comes from UK/US model rules.

## Key Question 4: Chamas in Kenya — scale, graduation from savings to businesses and property, governance lessons, table banking

### Takeaway
Kenya's chama sector is the most mature in Africa: roughly 300,000 groups holding about KES 300 billion (Ministry of Co-operatives figure, repeatedly cited 2016–2024), with FinAccess 2024 putting informal-group membership at about 30.7% of adults (8.6–8.7 million people). Graduation follows a ladder — merry-go-round → table banking → share-based investment chama → registered co-op/company/SACCO — and the governance lessons are concrete: written constitution, dual-signatory bank account, meeting-by-meeting financial reporting, audits, and a county registration under the Community Groups Registration Act 2022.

### Cited Findings

**Scale**
- FinAccess 2024: formal access 84.8% (2021: 83.7%); bank usage 52.5%; SACCO participation 11.7%; "informal group membership rose to 30.7 percent"; investment in securities 3.1%. — [CBK/KNBS/FSD Kenya, 2024 FinAccess Household Survey main report](https://www.centralbank.go.ke/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/2024-FINACCESS-HOUSEHOLD-SURVEY-MAIN-REPORT.pdf)
- Informal groups (ASCAs, ROSCAs, chamas) served 8.7 million adults in 2024, up from 7.8 million in 2021 (another passage of the same report gives 30.6% / 8.6 million vs 28.7% / 7.8 million). Overall informal usage 30.8% of adults; informal loans 38.1%; informal savings 23.6%. Rural informal-group usage rose to 32.3% while urban fell to 28.5%. 69.2% of group users belong to one group (72.1% in 2021), and the number in more than three groups "almost doubled." — [FinAccess 2024 main report](https://www.centralbank.go.ke/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/2024-FINACCESS-HOUSEHOLD-SURVEY-MAIN-REPORT.pdf)
- Gender: 40.9% of women take informal loans vs 35.2% of men; women's greater involvement in "savings groups or chamas, which offer greater flexibility" is cited. — [FinAccess 2024](https://www.centralbank.go.ke/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/2024-FINACCESS-HOUSEHOLD-SURVEY-MAIN-REPORT.pdf)
- Consumer protection: "new concerns emerged for Chama/groups, with 28.9 percent of users reporting unexpected charges in 2024." — [FinAccess 2024](https://www.centralbank.go.ke/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/2024-FINACCESS-HOUSEHOLD-SURVEY-MAIN-REPORT.pdf)
- "Estimated to be 300,000 chamas managing a total of KSH 300 billion (US$3.4 billion) in assets"; "one in three Kenyans is a chama member"; Kenya Association of Investment Groups (KAIG) had 1,800 registered groups holding over KES 469 billion (2016 figure). — [Wikipedia: Chama (investment)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chama_(investment)); Ministry of Co-operatives "over Sh300 billion" also cited in [Business Daily](https://www.businessdailyafrica.com/bd/corporate/enterprise/bank-s-chama-awards-to-showcase-power-of-group-investments-2070772)
- About 68% of chama members are women. — [Huduma Global](https://hudumaglobal.com/blog/understanding-chamas-kenya-investment-groups-merry-go-rounds-collective-saving)

**How they work and graduate**
- Types: merry-go-round (ROSCA; e.g. 12 members × KES 5,000/month → each gets KES 60,000 once a year, no interest); table banking (pool lent to members at 5–10%/month); investment chamas (real estate, stocks, bonds, businesses; "often register formally to facilitate property ownership"); welfare chamas. — [Huduma Global](https://hudumaglobal.com/blog/understanding-chamas-kenya-investment-groups-merry-go-rounds-collective-saving)
- Origins in the late 1980s–1990s "harambee" ethos, initially women-dominated; successful chamas "can formalize into Savings and Credit Cooperatives (SACCOs) or limited companies; some have evolved into banking institutions"; registered co-ops fall under the Sacco Societies Regulatory Authority. — [Wikipedia: Chama (investment)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chama_(investment))
- Registration routes: Community Groups Registration Act 2022 at county level (≥10 members, KES 1,000 fee, KES 100 annual filing) or LLP/limited company under the Companies Act. — [Huduma Global](https://hudumaglobal.com/blog/understanding-chamas-kenya-investment-groups-merry-go-rounds-collective-saving)
- Table banking (JoyWo model): monthly meetings; savings and repayments placed on the table and re-lent at once; 240,000+ members, KES 3.4 billion revolving, 44 counties (grown from 60 women in 2009). — [JoyWo](https://joywo.org/); [Kenyans.co.ke](https://www.kenyans.co.ke/news/118434-what-you-should-know-about-rachel-rutos-41billion-joyful-women-initiative)
- Nation guide on converting a merry-go-round into an investment vehicle. — [Nation: How to transform your chama from merry-go-round to an investment](https://nation.africa/kenya/business/how-to-transform-your-chama-from-merry-go-round-to-an-investment--4801072)

**Governance lessons and failures**
- Constitution should require at least two signatories for any withdrawal; counter embezzlement with "transparent record-keeping, regular audits, dual-signatory bank accounts, and mandatory financial reporting at every meeting." — [MoneyIssues.co.ke: How to write a chama constitution](https://moneyissues.co.ke/how-to-write-a-chama-constitution-in-kenya-step-by-step-guide-and-sample-template/)
- Successful chamas use "clear constitutions, dual-signatory bank accounts, transparent record-keeping, regular audits, and post-dated cheques to prevent member default and embezzlement." — [Huduma Global](https://hudumaglobal.com/blog/understanding-chamas-kenya-investment-groups-merry-go-rounds-collective-saving)
- Unregistered chamas face "embezzlement, mismanagement, loan default, and interpersonal conflicts"; in merry-go-rounds early recipients create dropout risk for later ones. — [Wikipedia: Chama (investment)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chama_(investment))
- Failure cases: a Machakos table-banking treasurer, suspected of embezzling KES 300,000 of members' year-long savings, took her own life after members demanded the money (2022). — [Nation](https://nation.africa/kenya/counties/machakos/chama-treasurer-commits-suicide-after-members-demand-sh300-000-savings-4059814); a Nairobi self-help-group official charged with defrauding members of KES 70,000 (Oct 2022–Mar 2023) after officials "stopped holding physical meetings and directed members to send their cash directly to the treasurer." — [Nation](https://nation.africa/kenya/counties/nairobi/-chama-treasurer-charged-with-defrauding-members-sh70-000-4286232); Kumisa Sacco (Kiambu) collapsed with KES 100 million of shareholders' money. — [Nation](https://nation.africa/kenya/news/kumisa-heist-kiambu-politician-cronies-wiped-off-sh100-million-from-youth-sacco-4117788)

### Inferences
- The FinAccess 2024 pattern — informal-group membership stable-to-rising (28.7% → 30.7%) even as formal inclusion hits 84.8% — shows chamas are complements to, not substitutes for, formal finance; Kenyans hold them as a portfolio alongside M-Pesa and banks.
- The Nairobi fraud case illustrates the single most reliable red flag in any group: suspension of physical meetings combined with instructions to pay one individual directly.
- The 28.9% "unexpected charges" figure is the first FinAccess evidence of fee opacity inside chamas themselves, which is a different failure mode from theft and argues for written fee schedules in the constitution.

### Gaps
- The "300,000 chamas / KES 300 billion" figure has been repeated since at least 2016 without an updated primary estimate; the FinAccess report gives membership, not asset values.
- FSD Kenya/KAIG recent (2023–2025) asset data was not accessible (People Daily article returned HTTP 403).

## Key Question 5: Nigeria's digital / fintech formalisation of ajo — platforms, regulators' positions, scale

### Takeaway
Two distinct digitisation tracks exist in Nigeria: (a) agent/collector-led platforms (AjoCard, Bankly, LibertyPay, Alajo App, AjoMoney, Thrifto, Sterling's OneBank "digital Ajo") that keep the alajo but give them POS/USSD, receipts and escrowed, CBN-licensed deposit accounts; and (b) consumer-app "group savings" (PiggyVest Group Target Savings, Cowrywise Circles) that pool goals but keep each member's money in their own wallet. Regulators have not written an "ajo rulebook"; instead CBN licenses the deposit-taking entity (MFB, super-agent, PSB) and warns against unlicensed operators under BOFIA 2020, while SEC regulates anything that is an investment scheme.

### Cited Findings

**Collector-led platforms**
- AjoCard: "a FinTech solution firm with a duly approved microfinance license by Central Bank of Nigeria (CBN)" with deposits insured by NDIC; "Personal Bankers facilitate daily savings collections for open-market retailers, digitizing the traditional ajo, adashe, esusu process with mPOS and membership cards." — [AjoCard (Google Play listing / company site)](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.ajocard.app.prod&hl=en_US&gl=NG); [ajocard.com](https://ajocard.com/)
- Bankly "digitises" ajo by giving collectors mobile receipts and USSD access for secure deposits. — [Technext, 29 Jul 2025](https://technext24.com/2025/07/29/fintech-reinventing-alajo-model-nigeria/)
- LibertyPay: introduced 150,000 digital Ajo cards in late 2023, reported ~150% growth in collection volumes post-launch, and projects ~₦5 billion (~$5.5m) in monthly group savings via cards and POS. — [Technext 2025](https://technext24.com/2025/07/29/fintech-reinventing-alajo-model-nigeria/)
- Alajo App: users had saved ₦373 million (~$443k) by April 2023; works over USSD on basic phones. — [Technext 2025](https://technext24.com/2025/07/29/fintech-reinventing-alajo-model-nigeria/)
- Sterling Bank's OneBank launched a digital Ajo scheme with monthly contributions and rotating lump-sum payouts; CircleFunds automates group creation and contribution tracking. — [Technext 2025](https://technext24.com/2025/07/29/fintech-reinventing-alajo-model-nigeria/)
- AjoMoney, founded 2021, digitises ROSCAs "known locally in Nigeria as Ajo, Esusu, or Adashe." — [TrustFinance: AjoMoney review](https://www.trustfinance.com/companies/ajo-money); Thrifto launched a platform to digitise ajo/esusu groups. — [TechCabal](https://techcabal.com/2022/10/11/mobile-money-is-surging-in-africa-as-more-disruption-occur/)
- Market context cited by Technext: ~14.6 million Nigerian adults (~15%) under-banked; 70% of market traders are women; adoption concentrated in Lagos and Kano; "traders previously burned by traditional alajo fraud have regained confidence through escrowed accounts and real-time monitoring." — [Technext 2025](https://technext24.com/2025/07/29/fintech-reinventing-alajo-model-nigeria/)
- Note: "Esusu" the US fintech (Forbes Fintech 50 2022, 5m+ users, $2.2bn valuation) is a rent-reporting company, not a Nigerian ajo platform, despite the name. — [Nairametrics, Jun 2022](https://nairametrics.com/2022/06/08/african-owned-startups-chipper-cash-and-esusu-make-forbes-fintech-50-list-of-2022/)

**Consumer-app group savings**
- PiggyVest Group Target Savings: save towards a shared goal "while keeping your funds in your own secure, private account," earning up to 12% annual interest; unlike traditional ajo "where everyone's money goes into a shared pot managed by one person," each member controls their own money; a leaderboard shows group progress but individual balances stay private. — [PiggyVest blog](https://blog.piggyvest.com/save/piggyvest-target-group-savings/)
- PiggyVest scale: over 5 million users by end-2024 and ₦835 billion paid out in 2024; crossed 6 million users and paid out ₦1.3 trillion in 2025 (+56%). — [Techpoint Africa, 2025 payouts](https://techpoint.africa/news/piggyvest-2025-payouts/); [Techpoint Africa, 2024 payouts](https://techpoint.africa/news/piggyvest-disburses-billions-users/)
- Cowrywise Circles: "Collections" (everyone saves, only the admin can withdraw) and "Challenges" (everyone withdraws at maturity); Cowrywise is built around SEC-regulated naira and dollar mutual funds. — [Dignited comparison](https://www.dignited.com/56190/piggyvest-vs-cowrywise-which-is-your-ideal-online-savings-platform/); [Auritrack comparison](https://www.auritrack.com/compare/cowrywise-vs-piggyvest)
- EFInA 2023: 38% of adults — 69% of those with a transaction account — saved formally; formal inclusion 64% (2020: 56%), banked 52% (58.3m adults), other formal 12% (13.3m), informal-only 10% (10.7m), excluded 26% (28.9m); informal-only fell from 14% in 2020; total informal usage (including those also formally served) rose from 33% (2020) to 46% (2023), "+39%." — [EFInA A2F 2023 presentation](https://efina.org.ng/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/A2F-2023-Event-Day-Presentation-Version4-1.pdf); summary in [NAN](https://nannews.ng/financial-inclusion-reaches-64-efina-survey-reveals/)

**Regulatory posture**
- CBN licenses fintechs across Switching & Processing, MMO, PSSP, PTSP, Super Agent, PSB and digital MFB categories; it "has begun licensing digital-only microfinance banks and super-agents, thereby enabling these fintech networks to operate with greater scope and oversight." — [Technext 2025](https://technext24.com/2025/07/29/fintech-reinventing-alajo-model-nigeria/); [Chambers Fintech 2025 Nigeria](https://practiceguides.chambers.com/practice-guides/fintech-2025/nigeria/trends-and-developments/O20012)
- April 2022 CBN circular warned Nigerians to "refrain from dealing with unlicensed or illegal financial operators, who lure and defraud…by offering extraordinary returns" and to verify licences on CBN/SEC websites; under BOFIA 2020 it is illegal to carry on banking or financial business without a CBN licence. — [Daily Trust: Early detection of unregistered financial institutions](https://dailytrust.com/early-detection-of-unregistered-financial-institutions-in-nigeria-mechanisms-gaps-and-global-lessons/)
- From January 2025 CBN holds banks accountable for fraud by debiting accounts that receive fraud proceeds; EFCC identified 58 illegal investment companies in March 2025; NDIC warns against "wonder banks" but lacks direct enforcement power. — [Daily Trust](https://dailytrust.com/early-detection-of-unregistered-financial-institutions-in-nigeria-mechanisms-gaps-and-global-lessons/)
- Ghana comparator: the Ghana Cooperative Susu Collectors Association has a Bank of Ghana mandate to supervise susu collectors, but BoG "has refrained both from attempting direct regulation of these numerous, small informal agents and from granting monopolistic licensing powers to their Association." — [World Bank: Rural and Micro Finance Regulation in Ghana](https://documents1.worldbank.org/curated/en/402221468749372797/pdf/266520AFR0wp49.pdf); susu collector fee = one day's contribution per ~31-day cycle (GHS 10/day × 31 = 310, saver gets 300). — [AfroTools susu guide](https://afrotools.com/blog/susu-savings-ghana/); [Wikipedia: Susu collectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susu_collectors)

### Inferences
- The two tracks solve different failure modes: collector-led platforms attack the alajo-absconds problem (escrow in a licensed MFB, receipts, GPS-tagged collections), while PiggyVest-style group savings eliminate the pooled pot altogether — which means they are not really ROSCAs (no lump-sum rotation, no credit), just social commitment devices.
- Because CBN regulates the entity (MFB/super-agent) rather than the product, a digital ajo's safety depends on whether customer money actually sits in a licensed, NDIC-insured institution; marketing language ("digitising ajo") is not itself a licence.
- The EFInA shift (informal-only down 14%→10%, but total informal use up to 46%) indicates Nigerians are layering formal accounts on top of ajo, not abandoning ajo — the same portfolio pattern as Kenya.

### Gaps
- No credible figures found for Moniepoint or OPay group-savings/ajo features; the Technext piece and comparison sites do not mention them. Treat claims about those two as unverified.
- No SEC or CBN document found that specifically addresses ROSCA/ajo digitisation as a product class; the regulatory basis is inferred from licensing categories and general anti-unlicensed-operator warnings.
- Cowrywise does not publish user numbers; Bankly/AjoMoney user and volume figures were not found.
- The cited "14.6 million under-banked adults" (Technext) does not match EFInA 2023's 10.7m informal-only / 28.9m excluded; the definitions differ and the Technext figure is unsourced.

## Key Question 6: Risks and failure patterns — absconding collectors, default cascades, ponzi schemes disguised as clubs/co-ops, and what protections work

### Takeaway
Nigerian losses to ponzi and related schemes are enormous relative to the informal savings they imitate — NDIC put cumulative losses at ₦911.45bn over 23 years (2022), SEC at ₦300.2bn for recent schemes (Oct 2025), and CBEX alone at ~₦1.3 trillion (2025) — and several schemes explicitly disguised themselves as cooperatives or investment clubs (Famzhi Interbiz). Genuine groups fail in three ways — the collector/treasurer absconds, early receivers stop paying (default cascade), and records are unenforceable — and the protections with evidence behind them are written constitutions, dual signatories, meeting-by-meeting reporting, guarantors for early positions, registered co-operative status, and keeping funds in a licensed deposit-taker.

### Cited Findings

**Fraud inside legitimate ROSCAs/thrift (Nigeria)**
- Lagos market survey (N=1,200; 2024): 82% said deviant practices in thrift collection are common (37.5% "quite common," 44.5% "common"); most prominent forms: misappropriation of funds 35%, non-payment of dues 35%, "exit frauds" (collector/scheme disappears before completing the cycle) 30%; 74.6% reported significant financial losses (44.6% "incredibly significant," 30% "significant"); 75% agree financial strain drives misconduct; peer influence 56%, lack of awareness 26%, cultural practices 18%; only 7.5% of traders described themselves as economically comfortable. Recommended: transparent record-keeping, oversight committees, multiple signatories, written agreements, dispute-resolution mechanisms. — [Adeyemi et al., ASMJ Jan 2025](https://www.gojamss.net/journal/index.php/ASMJ/article/viewFile/1285/1297)
- Typical pattern: a collector takes "a month's worth of contributions" and disappears during the Christmas season (case of Rose Ojoma); "without regulation, Ajo users risk losing their savings to fraud." — [VOA News, Dec 2024](https://www.voanews.com/a/nigerians-turn-to-community-savings-amid-financial-struggles-/7890615.html)
- Default cascade mechanism: in commercial bidding ROSCAs early recipients default at 2–3× the rate of late recipients (1.74% vs 0.54% pre-1993), and because every default must be covered, the organiser requires cosigners from early recipients. — [Klonner & Rai 2005](https://eml.berkeley.edu/~webfac/emiguel/e271_s06/klonner.pdf)

**Ponzi/pyramid schemes dressed as investment or cooperative (Nigeria)**
- NDIC (2022): Nigerians lost ₦911.45 billion to Ponzi and related fraud over the preceding 23 years. — [Technext, 30 Oct 2025](https://technext24.com/2025/10/30/ponzi-scheme-nigerians-lost-n300-2b-sec/)
- SEC (Oct 2025): ₦300.2 billion lost "in recent years" to schemes including MMM, Famzhi Interbiz and MBA Forex. — [Technext, 30 Oct 2025](https://technext24.com/2025/10/30/ponzi-scheme-nigerians-lost-n300-2b-sec/)
- Scheme-by-scheme (BusinessDay): MMM Nigeria — 30%/month promised, 3m+ users, ~₦18bn lost, crashed Dec 2016; Ultimate Cycler — 400% promised, ₦12,500 entry; Twinkas (2017) — 200%; D9 Club (2017) — "sports betting profits," >₦10bn; MBA Forex (2020) — ₦171bn, CEO Maxwell Odum declared wanted by EFCC; Famzhi Interbiz (2021) — up to 30%/month, ₦30bn, "posed as a cooperative," SEC declared it illegal; Brisk Capital (2021) — 30%/month, >₦2bn, youth-targeted via social media; Ovaioza Farm Produce Storage (2022) — up to 70%, >₦3bn, agri-investment disguise; Chinmark Group (2022) — >₦40bn, claimed hospitality/transport investments; CBEX — launched Jul 2024, crashed Apr 2025, "100 percent return…within 30 days," referral tiers, SEC: "unregistered and operated illegally." — [BusinessDay: From MMM to CBEX](https://businessday.ng/technology/article/from-mmm-to-cbex-the-chronicles-of-nigerians-ponzi-relationship/)
- CBEX "reportedly defrauded Nigerians of over ₦1.3 trillion." — [Daily Trust](https://dailytrust.com/early-detection-of-unregistered-financial-institutions-in-nigeria-mechanisms-gaps-and-global-lessons/); same figure in [allAfrica/Daily Trust, Apr 2025](https://allafrica.com/stories/202504160187.html)
- SEC has flagged lists of ponzi and illegal schemes and provides a verification link. — [Legit.ng](https://www.legit.ng/business-economy/economy/1661085-sec-flags-10-ponzi-illegal-investment-schemes-nigeria-releases-link-check-authenticity/); SEC DG warned against unregistered platforms and issued cease-and-desist orders, referring cases to EFCC. — [ICIR](https://www.icirnigeria.org/ponzi-scheme-sec-warns-of-growing-threat-to-nigerian-stock-market/)

**Legal framework (ISA 2025)**
- ISA 2025 (Section 196, definitions in Section 357) expressly prohibits Ponzi and pyramid schemes; promoters face a fine of not less than ₦20 million and/or up to 10 years' imprisonment; SEC may issue cease-and-desist orders, freeze accounts, and recover investigation costs. Definition: "any arrangement in which returns are paid to existing members from funds contributed by new members" or where earnings come primarily from recruitment. — [BusinessDay: SEC's role under ISA 2025](https://businessday.ng/bd-weekender/article/strengthening-investor-protection-in-nigeria-the-secs-role-in-mitigating-ponzi-schemes-under-the-isa-2025/); [Mondaq on ISA 2025](https://www.mondaq.com/nigeria/securities/1700288/the-investment-and-securities-act-2025-navigating-the-key-updates). Note: the BusinessDay MMM-to-CBEX article states "a N40 million fine"; the ISA-focused BusinessDay article and Mondaq say ₦20 million minimum — [conflict; the ₦20m figure appears in the more legally specific sources](https://businessday.ng/technology/article/from-mmm-to-cbex-the-chronicles-of-nigerians-ponzi-relationship/).
- Section 169 prohibits misuse of investor funds: ₦50 million or four times the unlawful gain, plus restitution. — [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/nigeria/securities/1700288/the-investment-and-securities-act-2025-navigating-the-key-updates)
- Verification: check registration at SEC's portal www.sec.gov.ng/cmos before transacting. — [BusinessDay](https://businessday.ng/bd-weekender/article/strengthening-investor-protection-in-nigeria-the-secs-role-in-mitigating-ponzi-schemes-under-the-isa-2025/)
- Detection gaps identified: reactive detection after schemes grow; capacity constraints; jurisdictional overlap between CBN/SEC/NDIC/EFCC; over-reliance on complaints; weak whistleblower incentives; poor public licence verification. — [Daily Trust](https://dailytrust.com/early-detection-of-unregistered-financial-institutions-in-nigeria-mechanisms-gaps-and-global-lessons/)

**Tell-tale signs of a ponzi vs a legitimate group (as given by sources)**
- Unrealistic promises of high, fixed returns with minimal risk; no SEC registration; aggressive social-media and referral marketing; dependence on continuous new-investor funds; absence of genuine business operations. — [BusinessDay / ISA 2025](https://businessday.ng/bd-weekender/article/strengthening-investor-protection-in-nigeria-the-secs-role-in-mitigating-ponzi-schemes-under-the-isa-2025/)
- Referral/pyramid structures with tiered bonuses based on network size; vague investment descriptions. — [BusinessDay: MMM to CBEX](https://businessday.ng/technology/article/from-mmm-to-cbex-the-chronicles-of-nigerians-ponzi-relationship/)

**Protections that work (cross-country)**
- Dual signatories; transparent records; regular audits; mandatory financial reporting at every meeting; post-dated cheques against default. — [MoneyIssues.co.ke](https://moneyissues.co.ke/how-to-write-a-chama-constitution-in-kenya-step-by-step-guide-and-sample-template/); [Huduma Global](https://hudumaglobal.com/blog/understanding-chamas-kenya-investment-groups-merry-go-rounds-collective-saving)
- Three-padlock cash box with keys held by three different members; five-officer committee; written constitution defining fines and share-cancellation on default. — [CARE VSLA 101](https://www.care.org/our-work/economic-growth/savings-groups/vsla-101/); [VSLA.net](https://www.vsla.net/the-vsla-methodology/)
- Guarantors/cosigners for early recipients and organiser-backed default guarantee in commercial ROSCAs. — [Klonner & Rai 2005](https://eml.berkeley.edu/~webfac/emiguel/e271_s06/klonner.pdf)
- Registered cooperative status (state Director of Cooperatives; bye-laws; 20% shareholding cap; returns filed). — [FurtherAfrica](https://furtherafrica.com/2020/11/19/establishing-a-cooperative-society-for-investment-purposes-in-nigeria/)
- Escrowed, NDIC-insured accounts in a CBN-licensed MFB for digital ajo. — [AjoCard](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.ajocard.app.prod&hl=en_US&gl=NG); [Technext](https://technext24.com/2025/07/29/fintech-reinventing-alajo-model-nigeria/)
- India: registered chits require a foreman security deposit, Registrar supervision of draws and arbitration; unregistered chits have "zero oversight…no legal recourse." — [chit.fund](https://chit.fund/what-is-chit-fund/)

### Inferences
- Structural distinction: a legitimate ROSCA/ASCA/club's payouts are bounded by what members put in (plus interest members paid each other or market returns on a disclosed portfolio); a ponzi's promised return exceeds any plausible source and is funded by new entrants — so "where does the return come from, and can I see the asset?" is the operative test. A group that pays "30% a month" cannot be a thrift society because no member pays 30%/month to borrow from it.
- The "posed as a cooperative" disguise works because state cooperative registration carries legitimacy but little prudential supervision; a cooperative that recruits non-members publicly for fixed returns has crossed into SEC territory.
- The Lagos finding that "non-payment of dues" is as prevalent as collector misappropriation (35% each) shows that member default, not just collector theft, is half the problem — the default-cascade risk identified theoretically in ROSCA literature.

### Gaps
- No Nigerian regulator has published an estimate of annual losses to informal ajo/esusu collector fraud (as distinct from ponzi schemes); available evidence is perceptual (Lagos survey) and anecdotal (VOA).
- Loss figures for ponzi schemes come from press and SEC statements and are not reconciled with one another (₦300bn SEC vs ₦911bn NDIC vs ₦1.3trn CBEX alone); treat as orders of magnitude.

## Key Question 7: What the evidence says — participation rates, default rates, implied returns (EFInA, FinAccess, World Bank/CGAP/IPA, academic)

### Takeaway
Participation is high and durable: ~46% of Nigerian adults used some informal financial service in 2023 (10% relied on informal only), ~30.7% of Kenyan adults belonged to an informal group in 2024, and the Lagos trader sample was universally enrolled. Hard default data exist only for commercially organised bidding ROSCAs (Shriram, India: ~1.6% total default) and for VSLAs via RCTs; implied returns range from zero (random ROSCA) through 2.7–7.4% p.a. (Philadelphia bidding ROSCA) to 5–15% annual dividends (Nigerian thrift co-ops) and double-digit monthly rates in table banking — with the IPA RCT finding real but modest welfare effects.

### Cited Findings

**Nigeria (EFInA A2F 2023)**
- Adults 18+: banked 52% (58.3m), other formal 12% (13.3m), informal only 10% (10.7m), excluded 26% (28.9m); financially included 74% (68% in 2020); formally included 64% (56% in 2020); ~40 million adults remain formally excluded; informal-only share fell from 14% (2020) to 10% (2023); exclusion down 10 points (~9 million fewer excluded adults). — [EFInA A2F 2023 presentation](https://efina.org.ng/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/A2F-2023-Event-Day-Presentation-Version4-1.pdf)
- Informal financial service uptake (any use): 29% (2016), 30% (2018), 33% (2020), 46% (2023) — "increased by 39% since 2020, with significant increase in the use of village associations predominantly in the South East"; drivers listed as savings groups, savings thrift, village associations, co-operatives, moneylenders and MFIs. — [EFInA A2F 2023](https://efina.org.ng/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/A2F-2023-Event-Day-Presentation-Version4-1.pdf)
- 38% of adults saved formally in 2023; savings uptake (any, incl. at home / with family) grew ~2.4% CAGR 2020–2023. — [EFInA A2F 2023](https://efina.org.ng/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/A2F-2023-Event-Day-Presentation-Version4-1.pdf)
- Ogun State study: 43.6% ROSCA participation in sample. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362900529_DETERMINANTS_OF_PARTICIPATION_IN_ROTATING_SAVINGS_AND_CREDIT_ASSOCIATIONS_ROSCAs_IN_OGUN_STATE_NIGERIA)

**Kenya (FinAccess 2024)**
- Informal group membership 30.7% (30.6% in another passage; 28.7% in 2021); 8.6–8.7m adults (7.8m in 2021); informal loans 38.1%, informal savings 23.6% of adults; rural 32.3% vs urban 28.5%; 28.9% of chama users reported unexpected charges. — [FinAccess 2024 main report](https://www.centralbank.go.ke/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/2024-FINACCESS-HOUSEHOLD-SURVEY-MAIN-REPORT.pdf)

**Default rates**
- Shriram Chits (India, 1992–1994 cohorts): total default 1.6% (customers other than finance companies); early-half recipients 1.74% vs late-half 0.54% before bid ceiling; 1.53% vs 0.73% after; total default 1.71% → 1.41% (-18%) after the 30% bid ceiling; defaults higher for larger-contribution groups, newer branches, remote and urban areas. — [Klonner & Rai 2005](https://eml.berkeley.edu/~webfac/emiguel/e271_s06/klonner.pdf)
- VSLA RCT: 68% of members borrowed; 61% completed a cycle; no explicit default rate reported in the summary. — [IPA](https://poverty-action.org/study/impact-savings-groups-lives-rural-poor-ghana-malawi-and-uganda)

**Implied returns / costs**
- Random ROSCA: organiser 0% loan; other members forgo interest (zero nominal return). — [Philadelphia Fed](https://www.philadelphiafed.org/-/media/frbp/assets/community-development/discussion-papers/discussionpaper-ROSCAs.pdf)
- Bidding ROSCA (Philadelphia, 2003–04): 2.74–7.44% p.a. borrowing cost for rounds 2–7. — [Philadelphia Fed](https://www.philadelphiafed.org/-/media/frbp/assets/community-development/discussion-papers/discussionpaper-ROSCAs.pdf)
- Indian chit worked example: 25 members × ₹4,000 for 25 months (₹1,00,000 pot); winning bid ₹88,000 (discount ₹12,000); foreman 5% = ₹5,000; ₹7,000 dividend = ₹292 per member, cutting the month's net instalment to ₹3,708. Statutory discount cap 30% (35% for 60–100-month chits, 40% for >100 months); foreman commission raised from 5% to 7% by the Chit Funds (Amendment) Act 2019; RBI may inspect foremen under Section 45N of the RBI Act; each state has a Registrar of Chits. — [chit.fund](https://chit.fund/what-is-chit-fund/); [Adwizr on flexible chits](https://adwizr.com/learn/chit-fund/registered-chit-funds-flexible-chits.html); [IndiaFilings on Chit Funds Act](https://www.indiafilings.com/learn/chit-funds); [InclusiveIAS](https://inclusiveias.com/chit-funds-upsc/)
- Indian sector size (industry site, unverified): 50m+ participants, ~₹3 lakh crore, 15,000+ registered operators. — [chit.fund](https://chit.fund/what-is-chit-fund/)
- Ghana susu collector: saver pays 1/31 of monthly savings (≈3.2%) for safekeeping, i.e. a negative return. — [AfroTools](https://afrotools.com/blog/susu-savings-ghana/)
- Nigerian thrift co-ops: 5–15% annual dividends on savings; 1%/month loans (blog source). — [KudiCompass](https://kudicompass.com/how-to-join-cooperative-society-nigeria/)
- Kenyan table banking: 5–10%/month on member loans; share-based chamas lend at up to 20%/month. — [Huduma Global](https://hudumaglobal.com/blog/understanding-chamas-kenya-investment-groups-merry-go-rounds-collective-saving); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chama_(investment))
- Digital group savings (PiggyVest): up to 12% p.a. — [PiggyVest blog](https://blog.piggyvest.com/save/piggyvest-target-group-savings/)

### Inferences
- The one rigorous default series (Shriram) shows that a well-screened, guarantor-backed ROSCA can run at ~1.5% default — comparable to or better than formal consumer credit — so the catastrophic losses in Nigerian ajo are a governance/custody problem, not an inherent feature of the model.
- Returns to members in traditional ROSCAs are negative-to-zero in cash terms (collector fee, no interest), which is consistent with the survey evidence that people join for credit access and commitment (43.5% credit, 39% savings in Lagos) rather than yield; the "return" is the lump sum and the discipline.
- The EFInA and FinAccess trends together (informal use rising alongside formal inclusion) imply digitisation efforts should layer custody and records onto groups rather than try to replace them.

### Gaps
- No Nigerian default-rate or loss-rate statistic for ajo/esusu/adashe from EFInA, CBN, NDIC or academic work was found; EFInA measures usage, not outcomes.
- I could not access the full EFInA 2023 report (only the launch presentation) or the FSD Kenya topline blog's chama section; some FinAccess numbers are internally inconsistent (30.6% vs 30.7%; 8.6m vs 8.7m), likely rounding across chapters.
- World Bank/CGAP papers specifically quantifying ROSCA/ASCA default or return rates for Nigeria were not located within the call budget; the Klonner–Rai Indian data and the IPA RCT are the strongest quantitative anchors found.
