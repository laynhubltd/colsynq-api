# Group-Investable Business Types and Documented Cases (Nigeria first, then Africa and global)

Scope note: Findings below come from Nigerian business press, platform disclosures, regulator statements, FSD Kenya/KAIG material and a small number of academic/industry studies. Where a return figure is a platform's own claim, a blog estimate, or a forum anecdote rather than an audited result, this is flagged explicitly. Two primary pages (TechCabal's ThriveAgric long-read and Estate Intel's cooperative-estates survey) returned HTTP 403 on fetch; their content is cited only to the extent it surfaced in search snippets.

## Key Question 1: Business categories groups commonly pool money into, and how each is typically structured, run and split

### Takeaway
Nigerian groups pool capital across a fairly standard menu: land/estate purchase through staff or association cooperatives; poultry, fish and crop ventures (directly or via crowd-farming platforms); hire-purchase vehicle fleets; POS/agent-banking outlets; filling stations and franchised QSRs at the top end; and, increasingly, passive instruments (T-bills, money-market funds, dollar assets) via SEC-registered apps. Across categories the dominant operating model is "members fund, a hired operator or platform runs, income split by fixed remittance or pro-rata units", and the main risk is not the business economics but counterparty/governance failure (theft, side-selling, title fraud, platform default).

### Cited Findings

**Sector-wide context**
- Nigeria has over 370,000 registered cooperative societies with more than 30 million members across the 36 states and FCT (Cooperative Federation of Nigeria, 2023) — [Nairametrics](https://nairametrics.com/2023/04/26/over-30-million-nigerians-belong-to-cooperative-societies/)

**Real estate (land banking, joint plots, estates, short-lets)**
- Staff cooperatives are the main Nigerian vehicle for group estate ownership: Chevron Employees Multipurpose Cooperative Society (CEMCS), Shell Lagos Staff Cooperative (COOPLAG) and TotalEnergies Staff Housing Cooperative (TEHCOOP) are named as notable players; Shell, Mobil and Total staff cooperatives were at one point jointly developing 200 housing units — [Estate Intel](https://estateintel.com/insights/real-estate-developments-owned-by-cooperative-societies-in-nigeria); [Estate Intel](https://estateintel.com/insights/co-operative-societies-of-shell-mobil-and-total-are-currently-developing-200-housing-units)
- Land in Ibeju-Lekki is described as reflecting 18–35% annual appreciation linked to the Dangote Refinery, Free Trade Zone and road projects (realtor/blog estimate, not audited) — [Baay Realty](https://baayrealty.com/developing-areas-buy-land-lagos/)
- Anecdotal land-banking claims: a plot bought for ₦1M in 2018 said to be worth ₦10–15M in some Ibeju-Lekki locations (realtor claim) — [Nigeria Real Estate](https://nigeria-real-estate.com/2026/06/09/land-banking-nigeria-buy-land-investment-avoid-fraud/)
- Omo-onile ("children of the landowner") extortion and sale of untitled or double-allocated plots are the dominant fraud risk in transition zones like Ibeju-Lekki — [ATLS Realtors](https://atlsrealtors.com/land-scams-in-lekki/); [Omonile Lawyer](https://omonilelawyer.com/tag/land-fraud-in-lagos/)
- Documented land-fraud case: over 500 subscribers paid an Ibeju-Lekki real-estate company for plots at Abijo in 2021 and none had been allocated a single plot years later — [Omonile Lawyer](https://omonilelawyer.com/ibeju-lekki-real-estate-company-scams-over-500-people-without-allocating-a-single-plot-to-any-buyer/)
- Short-lets: one Nigerian property blog claims Lagos short-lets averaged a 24% net yield in Q1 2026, with furnished 2–3-bed units in Lekki/Ikoyi/VI achieving 26–32% net after management and maintenance (blog figures, methodology not disclosed) — [Nigeria Real Estate Blog](https://nigeriarealestateblog.com/lagos-short-let-yields-q1-2026/); a more conservative source expects 8–12% returns "if managed well" — [HouseXplorer](https://housexplorer.com/blog/investing-in-short-term-rental-properties-airbnb-and-beyond-in-nigeria)
- Independent Airbnb data: median Lagos occupancy was 75% in Feb 2025–Jan 2026 per one tracker, while another tracker put platform-level occupancy at roughly 43% for the same window — [Airbtics](https://airbtics.com/annual-airbnb-revenue-in-lagos-nigeria/); contrasted with [AirROI](https://www.airroi.com/airbnb-data/nigeria/lagos/lagos)
- Utilities (electricity tariffs, diesel) add ₦250,000–₦700,000 per month per unit for a well-managed Lagos short-let — [PropComms Africa](https://propcommsafrica.com/the-2026-lagos-shortlet-reality-check-why-data-beats-hype-in-a-%E2%82%A6285-billion-market/)
- Fractional platform claims: Keble says it has onboarded 30,000+ users, 300,000+ fractional sales across 70+ properties, and that its 2024 projects delivered 20–30% capital appreciation plus rental income; it markets "up to 30%" annual returns on Naira properties and "up to 12%" on UK properties (platform claims) — [Nairametrics (sponsored)](https://nairametrics.com/2025/09/25/own-a-piece-of-dubai-london-the-usa-or-nigeria-starting-at-just-10-with-keble/); [TechCabal](https://techcabal.com/2025/01/17/keble-making-global-real-estate-accessible-to-africans-for-just-10/)

**Agriculture and agro-processing**
- Poultry capital: ₦350,000 for 50–100 chicks up to ₦45M+ for 5,000+ birds; 500 broilers ₦1.5–2.5M; 500 layers ₦3.5–4M (2026 guide estimates) — [Legit.ng](https://www.legit.ng/ask-legit/1709817-how-cost-start-a-chicken-farm-nigeria-everything-budget-for/); [Naijasabi](https://naijasabi.com.ng/how-to-start-poultry-farm-nigeria-2026/)
- Broiler cycle is 6–8 weeks; layers mature at 18–19 weeks and lay for up to ~72 weeks; feed is ~70% of production cost — [ChickenStarter](https://chickenstarter.com/choosing-between-layers-broilers-and-noilers/); [Agricbusiness](https://agricbusiness.com.ng/poultry-farming-how-profitable-is-poultry-business-in-nigeria/)
- Academic study (Delta State broiler operators): average net profit ₦928,720 per cycle, return per naira invested ₦2.89 (study period pre-2021, so nominal figures are dated) — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/348827505_FINANCIAL_BENEFITS_ANALYSIS_OF_BROILER_CHICKEN_FARM_OPERATORS_IN_DELTA_STATE_NIGERIA)
- Academic comparison: small-scale layer enterprise revenue ₦2,050,997 vs broiler ₦432,000 per cycle, layers more profitable — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Costs-and-returns-to-broiler-and-layer-enterprises-for-one-production-cycle_tbl3_337056128)
- Catfish: 500-fish tarpaulin setup ₦700,000–₦900,000 first cycle; 1,000-fish ₦1.3–4.0M; a ₦1.2M 500-fish operation estimated to yield ~₦400,000 profit in five months; feed is 50–75% of cost — [Toolbase](https://www.toolbase.com.ng/blog/catfish-farming-costs); [BusinessDay](https://businessday.ng/business-economy/article/how-to-profit-from-catfish-farming/); a 2024 study reported ₦3M invested yielding ₦700,000+ profit in six months — [WithinNigeria](https://www.withinnigeria.com/2026/06/24/catfish-farming-in-nigeria-profitable-business-guide-in-2026/)
- Outgrower model (Olam rice): farmers commit to buying inputs from Olam and selling all output to Olam; Olam provides training, pre-finance, inputs and marketing; buyback at prevailing market prices with payment within 48 hours; 35,000+ farmers engaged; farmer numbers scaled from 3,000 to 5,000 on 5,500 ha via the CBN Anchor Borrowers' scheme — [Daily Trust](https://dailytrust.com/how-out-grower-scheme-is-boosting-farmers-fortunes/); [Blueprint](https://blueprint.ng/olam-farm-targets-40000-tonnes-of-paddy-rice-by-2017/); Olam provided ₦536M interest-free finance to farmers — [BusinessDay](https://businessday.ng/agriculture/article/olam-provides-n536m-interest-free-finance-to-nigerian-farmers/)
- Babban Gona "agricultural franchise / mini-cooperative" model: farmers organised into trust groups, given inputs on credit, repaid at harvest, output aggregated for premium buyers; 200,000+ smallholders served, 98–99% repayment rate, yields up to 2x and income up to 3.5x national average (organisation and funder figures) — [Babban Gona](https://babbangona.com/); [Skoll](https://skoll.org/organization/babban-gona/); [FMO](https://www.fmo.nl/project-detail/60242)

**Transport and logistics (hire-purchase fleets)**
- Keke (tricycle) hire purchase: dealer HP typically needs 20–40% deposit; owner-hires-driver model yields ₦5,000–₦10,000 daily remittance, ₦125,000–₦250,000 gross monthly (25 days), owner costs ₦15,000–₦40,000/month, net ₦85,000–₦210,000/month; worked example: ₦1.3M vehicle, 30% deposit (₦390,000), ₦5,000/day over 18 months — [Turnet Finance](https://turnetfinance.ng/keke-tricycle-bike-business-in-nigeria/)
- Forum practice: weekly remittances of ₦18,000–₦20,000 for second-hand keke and ₦25,000–₦30,000 for new (Nairaland, anecdotal) — [Nairaland](https://www.nairaland.com/7030006/how-much-should-take-hire/2)
- Lagos danfo HP: drivers remit a fixed amount of fares to the owner and may take ownership after one to two years — [Harvard Epicenter](https://epicenter.wcfia.harvard.edu/blog/buses-and-bribes-lagos-shadowy-transit-network)
- Default is described as "a monster in the hire purchase business"; default leads to forfeiture of prior payments and repossession — [Financial Street](https://financialstreet.ng/the-world-of-hire-purchase-transporters-in-lagos/)
- Institutional fleet failures: LAGBUS managed 803 buses in 2012 but only ~200 were on the road daily, most broken down due to expensive contract maintenance; the 1977 Lagos State Transport Corporation (600+ buses) also failed and was closed — [Socialist Nigeria](https://www.socialistnigeria.org/2111/2013/03/01/ailing-lagbus-transport-scheme-the-failure-of-public-private-partnership-ppp/); [Vanguard](https://www.vanguardngr.com/2012/12/brt-breaking-the-cycle-of-failed-mass-intra-city-bus-schemes/)

**Money businesses (POS agents)**

### Inferences
- Ticket sizes cluster into tiers: ₦0.1–2M (POS outlet, 500-bird broiler batch, 500-fish pond, one used keke), ₦2–20M (layer farm, new keke/okada fleet of 3–10, catfish farm, single plot or half-plot in a peri-urban scheme), ₦40–400M (filling station, QSR franchise, short-let block, estate land for a cooperative). A 20-member group at ₦250,000 each reaches the first tier comfortably and the second with staggered contributions; the third tier needs 100+ members or institutional co-funding (which is why it is dominated by staff cooperatives).
- Split norms converge on two templates: (a) fixed remittance (keke/danfo driver pays a daily or weekly sum, owner bears repairs; outgrower sells at market price after input deduction); (b) pro-rata unit ownership with a manager/platform taking a fee (0.5% AUM at Risevest, 5% royalty + 4% levy at Chicken Republic, undisclosed spreads at crowd-farming platforms).
- The highest advertised returns (short-let 24–32%, Keble 30%, land 18–35%) are all unaudited realtor/platform figures, whereas the sober academic and bank-pooled instruments (T-bills 15–20%, broiler return of ₦2.89 per ₦1) are documented; the gap between the two is itself a risk signal.

### Gaps
- No reliable Nigerian data found on landlord/manager fee splits for jointly owned rental property, on clinic/school/laundromat/event-centre economics, on bureau de change economics, or on hotel/lounge returns; these would need trade-association or PwC/KPMG sector reports that were not retrieved.
- No audited default or repayment statistics for keke/okada hire-purchase fleets; evidence is anecdotal (Nairaland, blogs).
- Current post-deregulation per-litre fuel margins were not found; the ₦5 figure is pre-2023.
- FAO/IFAD outgrower literature and PwC/KPMG real-estate outlooks were not retrieved within the tool budget.

## Key Question 2: Documented case studies with figures (success and failure)

### Takeaway
Successes are overwhelmingly long-horizon, member-governed cooperatives with real assets (Chevron staff cooperative's 57-ha Nicon Town, Nigeria Immigration staff estates, Kenya's Mwalimu Sacco, Mondragón); failures cluster where ordinary savers handed money to an operator promising fixed short-tenor returns (crowd-farming platforms with >90% default, MMM, MBA Forex, CBEX).

### Inferences
- Every Nigerian success case has (a) members with a common employer/profession (Chevron, Immigration, teachers), (b) a tangible asset with legal title (land/housing), and (c) a horizon of 5–20 years. Every Nigerian failure case has (a) strangers recruited by marketing, (b) a promised fixed return over 1–9 months, and (c) no asset the investor could seize.
- ThriveAgric vs Farmcrowdy/Crowdyvest shows that the same business model can end in full repayment or ₦7.7bn of unpaid debt depending on balance sheet, governance response and investor backing; the model itself (retail money funding seasonal farm cycles with insurance that excludes systemic risk) is fragile.
- The Fagor case is the clearest documented illustration of how a cooperative federation socialises failure (job redeployment, internal insurance), something no Nigerian group structure found here replicates.

### Gaps
- Could not access Estate Intel's full survey (403), so unit counts, member pricing and financing structure for Shell/Mobil/Total cooperatives are missing.
- No public data on CEMCS's acquisition price, member allocation price, or current member valuations.
- No portfolio-level return or exit data for Lagos Angel Network.
- No documented case of a Nigerian church/alumni/market-traders' association cooperative with audited figures was found within the tool budget.
- No named, figures-backed Kenyan chama (as opposed to a Sacco) success case was found; Money254 explicitly gives none.

## Key Question 3: Platforms enabling group investment in Nigeria today and their track record

### Takeaway
Since the SEC's January 2021 crowdfunding rules, only a handful of portals are registered (MonieWorx, Propcrowdy, PennyTree, Obelix 4.1.1 among those named), while the pre-2021 agri-crowdfunding cohort largely defaulted; mainstream apps (Risevest, Cowrywise, Bamboo, Chaka, Trove, Keble, Cowa) are SEC-registered fund managers/sub-brokers rather than crowdfunding portals and carry no comparable default record.

### Cited Findings

- Named SEC-approved portals (Sept 2025): MonieWorx (₦5.23bn raised, 10,000+ investors, claimed zero defaults), Propcrowdy (real estate, 20,000+ investors, founded by Roland Igbinoba), PennyTree, Obelix 4.1.1 Alternative Ltd — [Nairametrics](https://nairametrics.com/2025/09/20/meet-the-owners-of-nigerias-sec-approved-crowdfunding-platforms/)

- For a group, the regulated-portal route (MonieWorx, Propcrowdy) gives SEC trust-account protection but a thin track record (post-2021 only); the fund-manager apps give liquidity and audited underlying instruments but are individual accounts, so the "group" layer must be handled by the members themselves or via Cowa-type group tooling.

- No independent (non-platform) data on Propcrowdy or Keble payout history or default rates.

## Key Question 5: Selection framework for a group

### Takeaway
The evidence supports a simple decision rule: match the asset class to the group's size, horizon and willingness to supervise; insist on legal title or an SEC trust account; cap any single operator-promised return at roughly the T-bill yield; and build in the governance practices that FSD Kenya/KAIG and the Nigerian cooperative successes share (common bond, written rules, elected officers, audited records).

### Cited Findings
- FSD Kenya finds 13% of chamas suffer theft/embezzlement and most collapse within two years; the cited cures are transparent records, proper leader selection and clear rules on contribution deadlines — [Money254 citing FSD Kenya/KAIG](https://www.money254.co.ke/post/chama-revolution-what-successful-chamas-know-do-why-many-fail)
- Mondragón's model allocates 85% of member capital to individual, interest-bearing, withdrawable accounts and 15% to collective reserve, and re-invests 60% of profits — [ILSR](https://www.ilsr.org/wp-content/uploads/files/images/mondragon.pdf)
- SEC crowdfunding rules require portals to hold investor funds in trust accounts and conduct due diligence on fundraisers — [Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/nigeria/financial-services/1447774/legal-framework-for-crowdfunding-in-nigeria)
- Default in hire purchase leads to forfeiture and repossession; operators go to lengths (even borrowing) to avoid it — [Financial Street](https://financialstreet.ng/the-world-of-hire-purchase-transporters-in-lagos/)
- Insurance on crowd-farming excluded systemic (COVID) risk and covered only on-farm events — [Techpoint](https://techpoint.africa/2020/10/06/thrive-agric-defaulting-insurance/)
- Chicken Republic requires a hands-on owner-operator devoting full-time effort — [FBDS](https://fbdsnigeria.com/directory/chicken-republic/)
- Land-fraud red flags: untitled plots, double allocation, omo-onile fee demands, non-allocation after payment — [ATLS Realtors](https://atlsrealtors.com/land-scams-in-lekki/); [Omonile Lawyer](https://omonilelawyer.com/ibeju-lekki-real-estate-company-scams-over-500-people-without-allocating-a-single-plot-to-any-buyer/)

### Inferences (framework)
1. Capital size: under ₦5M → POS, one broiler/catfish cycle, one used keke, or pooled T-bills/MMF; ₦5–50M → layer farm, 3–10 keke/okada under HP, a titled plot or two, a short-let unit; ₦50M+ → filling station, franchise, estate land, multiple short-lets — but only with a hired professional manager and a formal cooperative/company structure.
2. Members' skills: if nobody can supervise farming, driving or hospitality weekly, choose passive instruments or a regulated portal; operator-dependent businesses (farms, fleets, QSR) failed in the evidence when supervision was outsourced to a platform.
3. Time commitment: passive (T-bills, MMF, REIT/fractional) → monthly meeting; semi-active (keke fleet, POS, short-let) → weekly collection and inspection; active (farm, franchise, filling station) → a full-time manager and an audit committee.
4. Risk appetite: use the T-bill yield (15–20%) as the hurdle; anything promising more than roughly 2x that per year without a seizable asset should be treated as speculative; anything promising monthly double-digit returns matches the MMM/MBA/CBEX pattern.
5. Liquidity: MMF/T-bills (days) > listed stocks/dollar apps (days–weeks) > keke/POS (months to sell) > farm cycles (locked for the cycle) > land/estates (years). Groups with members who may need to exit should hold a liquid tranche.
6. Governance: adopt a common bond (employer, profession, congregation), written bye-laws, elected officers with term limits, dual-signatory accounts, individual capital accounts (Mondragón-style), and annual external audit — the features shared by CEMCS, NIMCOS, Mwalimu and Mondragón and absent from every failure case above.
7. Legal wrapper: register as a cooperative society (state Ministry of Commerce/Cooperatives) for estates and farms, or use an SEC-registered group tool (Cowa) or portal (MonieWorx/Propcrowdy) for securities; verify SEC registration directly rather than trusting certificates (CBEX forged them).

### Gaps
- No Nigerian academic or regulator study quantifying failure rates of Nigerian cooperatives/esusu/ajo groups by cause (the 13% embezzlement figure is Kenyan).
- No Nigeria-specific guidance document from SEC/CBN on how informal investment clubs should structure themselves was found.
