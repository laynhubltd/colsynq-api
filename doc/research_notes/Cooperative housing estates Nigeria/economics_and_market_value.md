# Cooperative housing estates in Nigeria: economics and market value

Conventions used below: "asking" = portal listing/asking price (not a closed transaction); "loan/unit" = FMBN loan amount divided by unit count (a cost proxy, not the member sale price). All naira figures nominal. Research date: 8 Oct 2026.

## Housing market context: deficit, supply, urbanisation, mortgage penetration, affordability, cost inflation, FX

### Takeaway
Nigeria's "deficit" is a contested number (14.9m–28m depending on author and year), but every source agrees formal supply (~50,000–100,000 units/yr) is an order of magnitude below need (700,000–820,000/yr), mortgages are ~0.5–0.6% of GDP, Lagos house prices are ~19x income, and cement has risen 330–500% since 2019–21 while the naira lost ~55% of its dollar value in 2023–24.

### Cited Findings
Deficit estimates (who/when):
- World Bank, Feb 2015: deficit 17 million units, ₦59.5 trillion to bridge (≈₦3.5m/unit implied), annual need 820,000 units (9 units per 1,000 population) — [Daily Trust](https://dailytrust.com/over-n59-5-trillion-required-to-bridge-nigeria-s-17-million-housing-deficit-world-bank/)
- Minister Fashola, March 2019, disputed the 17m figure as unvalidated; World Bank 2021 estimate 15.56m; a World Bank policy paper projected 20m by 2030; FG in 2023 put deficit at 28m with ₦21tn funding need; the National Housing Data Technical Committee in 2025 pegged it at 14.925m — [search summary across Guardian/NESG/CBN sources](https://guardian.ng/news/fg-puts-nigerias-housing-deficit-at-14-9-million/) (Guardian page returned 403 on fetch; figures per search snippet), [NESG White Paper Apr 2024](https://app.nesgroup.org/download_resource_documents/Bridging%20the%20Housing%20and%20Urban%20Development%20Divide._1713876197.pdf)
- CAHF Africa Housing Finance Yearbook 2024 (Nigeria chapter, Dr Roland Igbinoba): deficit "doubled from 14 million in 2010 to approximately 28 million in 2023"; "more than 28 million units" — [CAHF 2024 Nigeria PDF](https://housingfinanceafrica.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/NIGERIA.pdf)
- Lagos State Government (May 2025) cites a national deficit of 22m while reporting 9,970 units delivered — [allAfrica](https://allafrica.com/stories/202505150353.html)
- Lagos-specific deficit: 3.4 million units; annual need 227,576 units (RIRFHUD 2025, cited in IJRISS 2026 review); Punch 7 Jul 2025 "Lagos delivers 2.5m housing units amid 3.4m deficit" — [IJRISS 2026 systematic review PDF](https://rsisinternational.org/journals/ijriss/uploads/vol10-iss6-pg3334-3360-202606_pdf.pdf)

Mortgage penetration:
- Mortgages-to-GDP 0.60% (2021); residential mortgages outstanding US$2,166m (2021); 24,654 residential mortgages outstanding (2023, FMBN); 32 primary mortgage banks; typical non-subsidised mortgage rate 18%; average lending rate 12.33% (2022); mortgage sizes ₦5m–₦5bn; FMBN has given mortgages to <1% of 5.47m NHF contributors since 1997 — [CAHF 2024](https://housingfinanceafrica.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/NIGERIA.pdf)

Affordability:
- Lagos price-to-income ratio 19.2x (vs Cairo 18.4, Nairobi 11.5, Cape Town 5.4, Durban 4.2); at 9.75%/20yr/10% equity, low-income can afford ~₦2.46m homes, middle-income ₦5.3–17.6m; <5% of supply priced below ₦15m vs ~55% of demand; ~55% of supply above ₦200m vs ~5% of demand; rents up 80–120% 2024–2026 vs wages +7–9% (GTI Research, "Beyond Rent", article 25 Aug 2026) — [BusinessDay](https://businessday.ng/companies/article/lower-income-households-priced-out-of-lagos-housing-market-research/)

### Inferences
- Formal supply of 50,000–100,000 units/yr against 700,000–820,000 need implies 7–14% of annual need is met formally; the rest is informal/incremental self-build, which is the space cooperatives mostly operate in (land + incremental build).
- With a 19.2x price-to-income ratio and mortgages at 18–30%, unsubsidised mortgage-led ownership is irrelevant for most members; cooperative land-banking is one of very few feasible entry routes, which explains why cooperatives are "most active in land acquisition" (see Delivery section).
- Naira depreciation of ~58% between 2023 avg and mid-2024, plus 330–500% cement inflation since 2021, mean any cooperative estate priced in 2021–22 is now far below replacement cost: this is the main source of "paper appreciation" for members allocated pre-2023.

## Price benchmarks 2024–2026: land, units, rents and yields by city

### Takeaway
Lagos land ranges from ~₦3.5m/plot (Ikorodu median asking) to ₦600m–1.5bn (Lekki Phase 1); Abuja from ~₦25–30m (Karu/Kuje median) to ₦350m (Maitama); Port Harcourt median ₦30m and Ibadan ₦3.5m. Net residential yields are ~4–8% in Lagos (highest in Yaba/Surulere/Ikeja GRA, lowest Ajah/Ikoyi), ~3–6% in Abuja, ~3–4% in Port Harcourt; gross yields quoted at 6–8% nationally. Almost all of this is asking-price data.

### Inferences
- The cooperative-estate corridors (Ibeju-Lekki/Epe, Ikorodu, Kuje/Gwagwalada/Lugbe, Karsana) sit at ₦3.5m–₦50m per plot asking: 1–2 orders of magnitude below prime Lekki/Maitama. This is where member prices of ₦2.6m–₦9.5m/unit (next section) are plausible only with FMBN-type concessional finance or pre-2023 land.
- Net yields of 3–8% vs T-bills at 16–19% mean rental housing in Nigeria is held for capital appreciation and inflation hedging, not income; the same logic applies to members holding allocated plots rather than building.

## Cooperative estate pricing and appreciation: member prices vs later market values

### Takeaway
The only well-documented long-run case is CEMCS Nicon Town (≈₦5m plots c.2001 → ₦160–200m valuation in 2022 → ~₦1.4bn average asking for land in Oct 2026), implying ~18–19% nominal CAGR to 2022 and ~25% to 2026 asking (≈12–13% in USD). FMBN-financed cooperative estates for public servants price at ₦2.6m–₦9.5m per unit (2019–2026), far below market, but no post-allocation resale data exists for them.

- NNPC cooperative: ₦806m loss, members' savings trapped in an unfinished project (Farouk et al. 2014, cited) — [IJRISS 2026](https://rsisinternational.org/journals/ijriss/uploads/vol10-iss6-pg3334-3360-202606_pdf.pdf)

### Inferences
- Nicon Town CAGR (my arithmetic on cited figures): ₦5m (2001) → ₦160–200m (2022) = 17.9–19.2% nominal p.a. over 21 years; → ₦1.4bn average asking (2026) = ~25% p.a. over 25 years. In USD (₦~102–111/$ in 2001 vs ₦1,371 avg 2026): ~$45–49k → ~$1.02m, ≈12–13% p.a. For comparison the 364-day T-bill averaged 18.7% (2008–2026). So on a nominal naira basis the plot roughly matched/beat T-bills only on the 2026 asking price, and clearly beat USD/dollar deposits; the "2,000%+" headline is pre-2023-devaluation.
- Nicon Town is not representative: it was a completed, titled, IOC-backed estate on the Lekki corridor bought in bulk from a distressed insurer. The IJRISS review explicitly notes cooperatives that broke the cycle "are those backed by international oil companies."
- Discount to market at allocation: WoodHill ₦7.5–9.5m per built bungalow vs Kuje bare-land median ₦30m (2026 asking) implies allocation at a fraction of current market; Police ₦2.6–7.8m loan/unit and FMBN ₦4.6–8.1m loan/unit are similar. But these are construction-cost-based prices from 2018–2021 money; replacement cost has since risen ~300%.
- COOPLAG resale asking of ₦75–190m per house is consistent with Lekki-corridor pricing, suggesting Shell members captured most of the uplift individually (open resale), not the society.

### Gaps
- No documented original member prices for COOPLAG Gardens, NIMCOS, Customs or teachers' estates; no post-allocation resale data for any FMBN-financed cooperative estate, so no CAGR can be computed for the affordable segment.
- The ₦5m Nicon Town entry price is a secondary (blog/agent) figure; no CEMCS document or transaction record was found.
- The Estate Intel cooperative-developments article (the likeliest consolidated source) returned HTTP 403.
- Akacare: ₦997m loan vs 77 units × ₦7.5–9.5m (≈₦650m sales) — the loan likely covered more than Phase 1; unresolved.

## Delivery outcomes: share of subscribers who get a house/plot, time to allocation, abandonment

### Takeaway
Peer-reviewed Lagos studies put the member "success rate" at 27.2% (Oloke et al. 2017) and 38.3% aggregate across procurement methods (Ikeja study, 97 cooperatives, 2017); cooperatives are far better at acquiring land than at converting it to houses. No study quantifying time from subscription to allocation or an abandonment rate was found; FMBN-financed cooperative output is a few thousand units cumulatively.

### Cited Findings
- "Only 27.2% of cooperative members successfully achieve their housing objectives (Oloke et al., 2017)"; only 36% of Lagos cooperatives prepare regular financial statements; governance deficiencies explain 53.89% of variance in cooperative housing outcomes (Abdulkareem et al. 2026); Lagos cooperatives hold ~₦33bn members' capital and >2 million members (Lagos State Ministry of Commerce 2022) "yet this substantial pool of funds has produced" little housing; 40 studies reviewed (2009–2026), 75% cross-sectional, no longitudinal studies — [IJRISS systematic review, Jun 2026](https://rsisinternational.org/journals/ijriss/uploads/vol10-iss6-pg3334-3360-202606_pdf.pdf)
- "Empirical Assessment of Success Rate of Co-operative Societies' Housing Provision in Lagos State" (2017): members and principal officers of 97 institution-based cooperatives in Ikeja; procurement via land acquisition, housing development, loans, materials purchase, complete-house purchase; "aggregate 38.3% success rate of the different approaches combined", described as "not impressive" vs UN 8–10 units/1,000 population — [AGRIS/FAO record](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/66d9bb26f995ef9c0425aa1a); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/319321364_Empirical_Assessment_of_Success_Rate_of_Co-operative_Societies'_Housing_Provision_in_Lagos_State_Nigeria) (403 on fetch)
- IOP Lagos study (2020): systematic random sample of 401 cooperative leaders; "co-operative societies are most active in land acquisition activities but their capacity to convert these lands to housing units is limited"; "as constrained as other housing suppliers in meeting transaction costs"; abstract states cooperatives "account for up to thirty percent of the estimated seventeen million housing deficits" (phrasing ambiguous) — [IOP Conf. Ser. 588 052064](https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1088/1755-1315/588/5/052064)
- Tertiary-institution cooperatives in Lagos (Constraints study): 52% of responding members had financed houses to completion, 28% still under construction — [ResearchGate (via search snippet)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/315999700_Constraints_of_affordable_housing_through_cooperative_societies_in_tertiary_institutions_in_Lagos_State_Nigeria)
- FUTA Akure survey 2009 (297 staff): 92% had applied to credit societies for housing loans, ~57% of applications granted; 39% applied once, 25.5% twice, 16.5% three times, 10.8% four times; 73% of societies funded ≥21 houses each; 67% of sample rented, 20% owned — [Adedeji & Olotuah, ESJ 2012](https://eujournal.org/index.php/esj/article/download/181/185)
- Oyo State cooperatives mobilised ~₦146m (US$974k) for housing over ten years — [Academia.edu Oyo study](https://academia.edu/5372965/HOUSING_CO-OPERATIVES_Housing_Co-operative_Societies_and_Sustainable_Housing_Delivery_in_Oyo_State_Nigeria._Introduction) (via snippet)
- Structural frictions: C of O costs ₦3–10m per parcel and 12–24 months; ~90% of Lagos land untitled (World Bank 2023); self-provided trunk/internal infrastructure ₦20–55m per acre, adding 20–30% to project cost; land acquisition timelines "extend across several years, during which costs escalate" — [IJRISS 2026](https://rsisinternational.org/journals/ijriss/uploads/vol10-iss6-pg3334-3360-202606_pdf.pdf)
- FMBN CHDL output: ~40 ongoing projects, ~2,570 units projected (2021–22) — [ThisDay 2021](https://www.thisdaylive.com/2021/08/11/how-fmbn-is-leveraging-co-operatives-to-make-homes-possible-for-nigerians/); 664 units/9 projects for ₦5.4bn (2019) — [DMarketForces](https://dmarketforces.com/the-federal-mortgage-bank-of-nigeria-has-released-about-n5-4-billion-to-some-cooperative-societies/); FMBN total units financed (all products): 2,165 (2024), 6,911 (2025); project loan disbursements ₦31.5bn (2024), ₦79bn (2025); rent-to-own 367 beneficiaries ₦7.1bn — [Leadership](https://leadership.ng/fmbn-finances-6911-housing-units-in-2025-seeks-stronger-partnerships-to-tackle-housing-deficit/)
- Historic NHF performance: 2000–2005 FMBN disbursed ₦4.22bn of ₦15.4bn collected to individuals, ~4,619 units (≈₦913k each), ~385 units/yr — [ESJ 2012](https://eujournal.org/index.php/esj/article/download/181/185)

### Inferences
- The two Lagos success-rate figures (27.2% and 38.3%) measure "achieved housing objective" and "success of procurement approach" respectively, not strictly "received a plot"; both imply roughly 6–7 in 10 members do not get what they subscribed for.
- FMBN's cumulative cooperative-loan output (hundreds to low thousands of units per year) is <1% of national annual need; cooperative estates are materially significant only for the specific institution's staff, not for aggregate supply.

### Gaps
- The 24–43% range mentioned in the brief could not be sourced to a specific study; the 43% found relates to rent-to-income, not allocation.
- No study quantifying median years from subscription to allocation, nor an abandonment rate for cooperative estates, was found; the IJRISS review confirms no longitudinal studies exist.
- The IOP study's "30% of the 17m deficit" claim could not be checked against the full text.

## Cooperative share of supply and policy role (National Housing Policy, Renewed Hope)

### Takeaway
No credible aggregate exists for cooperatives' share of Nigerian formal housing delivery; FMBN's cooperative loan window and Renewed Hope "cooperative subscriptions" put cooperative-channel output in the low thousands of units per year against ~50,000–100,000 formal completions, i.e. a few percent at most, though cooperatives are a major channel for land (not house) access among the 2m+ Lagos members.

### Cited Findings
- FMBN Cooperative Housing Development Loan (launched c.2018): up to ₦500m per cooperative, 10% interest, up to 24-month tenor with 12-month moratorium; ~40 projects/2,570 units projected (2021–22) — [ThisDay](https://www.thisdaylive.com/2021/08/11/how-fmbn-is-leveraging-co-operatives-to-make-homes-possible-for-nigerians/); [BusinessDay](https://businessday.ng/features/article/understanding-how-fmbns-loan-products-enable-access-to-affordable-housing/)
- Renewed Hope Cities and Estates: ₦127bn budget to build 100,000 units with private developers; 500–1,000 units per site in zones/FCT, 250 per site in other states (CAHF 2024) — [CAHF 2024](https://housingfinanceafrica.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/NIGERIA.pdf); Phase 1 50,000 units; 34,500 units announced to start — [Estate Intel](https://estateintel.com/insights/the-federal-government-announces-plans-to-commence-construction-of-34500-housing-units-under-renewed-hope-housing-scheme-by-december); FMBN pledged ₦100bn — [ThisDay via PressReader](https://www.pressreader.com/nigeria/thisday/20240425/281526526117575); FMBN financing 3,139 Renewed Hope units (Ibeju-Lekki ₦27bn, Karsana ₦19.9bn, PH ₦10bn, Enugu ₦7.8bn) — [Leadership](https://leadership.ng/fmbn-finances-6911-housing-units-in-2025-seeks-stronger-partnerships-to-tackle-housing-deficit/)
- Cooperative channel inside Renewed Hope: 430 units handed to NMDPRA staff/cooperative members on 23 Sep 2026 (359 at Karsana, Abuja; 14 at Janguza, Kano); NMDPRA, NUPRC, SEC and NRS covered staff equity contributions outright; "over 100,000" expressions of interest — [ThisDay 8 Oct 2026](https://www.thisdaylive.com/2026/10/08/over-100000-eye-renewed-hope-cities-initiative-2/); [FMHUD](https://fmhud.gov.ng/read/3121); FMBN processed ₦8.3bn mortgages for the NMDPRA allocation — [Nigeria Housing Market](https://www.nigeriahousingmarket.com/news/430-nmdpra-workers-housing-allocations-fmbn-n8-3bn-mortgages)
- Lagos cooperatives: >2 million members and ~₦33bn capital (Lagos Ministry of Commerce 2022) — [IJRISS 2026](https://rsisinternational.org/journals/ijriss/uploads/vol10-iss6-pg3334-3360-202606_pdf.pdf)
- Government public housing historically completed only ~14% of planned units; private/financial-sector contribution "equally negligible" — [ABU thesis (via snippet)](https://kubanni-backend.abu.edu.ng/server/api/core/bitstreams/c2d77654-a986-4b2d-9617-956166fa33da/content)
- LagosHOMS has allocated over 20,000 units since inception (Lagos Mortgage Board) — [Nigeria Housing Market](https://www.nigeriahousingmarket.com/news/lagoshoms-allocates-over-20000-housing-units-since-inception) (headline only)

### Inferences
- Taking FMBN cooperative-channel output (~600–2,500 units/yr in recent years) plus Renewed Hope cooperative handovers (hundreds) against 50,000–100,000 formal completions suggests cooperatives account for roughly 1–5% of formal housing units, but a far larger (unquantified) share of serviced/unserviced plot access for salaried workers.
- Policy treats cooperatives as an off-take/aggregation channel (demand bundling, equity collection) rather than as developers; the NMDPRA case where the employer paid members' equity shows the model works best where the sponsoring institution subsidises entry.

### Gaps
- No official statistic splitting Nigerian housing completions by developer type (cooperative vs private vs government) exists in any source found; the ₦8.3bn/430-unit NMDPRA figure implies ~₦19m mortgage per unit but unit prices were not published.
- National Housing Policy text on cooperatives was not retrieved.

## Economics for the cooperative: surplus, capture of land appreciation, resale rules, opportunity cost

### Takeaway
Nigerian law restricts surplus distribution to what bye-laws and the committee approve, but there is no published evidence on cost-plus margins or surplus earned by cooperatives on estates; the two documented IOC estates (Nicon Town, COOPLAG) show appreciation accruing to individual members through open resale, while FMBN-financed estates sell at roughly cost. The opportunity cost benchmark is 16–19% on T-bills (2024–26) and 10% on FMBN cooperative loans.

### Cited Findings
- Nigerian Co-operative Societies Act: a registered society "shall not pay a dividend or bonus or otherwise distribute any part of its net surplus except as provided under the Act and until the proposal payment or distribution has been approved by the committee"; on liquidation, surplus distributed per bye-laws — [FAO copy of Act](https://faolex.fao.org/docs/pdf/nig120579.pdf); [Djetlawyer summary](https://djetlawyer.com/nigerian-co-operative-societies-act/)
- Cooperatives "pooling contributions to develop apartments and estates... while providing an additional stream of income" (Estate Intel) — [Estate Intel](https://estateintel.com/insights/real-estate-developments-owned-by-cooperative-societies-in-nigeria) (snippet)
- COOPLAG houses openly listed for resale at ₦75–190m (Oct 2026) and Nicon Town land at ~₦1.4bn average asking: resale is at market with no visible price cap — [NPC Cooplag](https://nigeriapropertycentre.com/for-sale/houses/lagos/lekki/showtype?n=cooplag+gardens+estate); [NPC Nicon Town](https://nigeriapropertycentre.com/for-sale/land/lagos/lekki/nicon-town/showtype)
- FMBN CHDL cost of funds 10%, 24 months; WoodHill sold at ₦7.5–9.5m (NHF contributors only) — [ThisDay](https://www.thisdaylive.com/2021/08/11/how-fmbn-is-leveraging-co-operatives-to-make-homes-possible-for-nigerians/); [Guardian](https://guardian.ng/property/fg-launches-national-cooperative-housing-development-scheme/)
- Opportunity cost: 364-day T-bill average 18.66% (2008–Mar 2026), 15.9–18.5% in 2026 — [CEIC](https://www.ceicdata.com/en/nigeria/policy-interbank-and-treasury-bills-rate/treasury-bill-rate-364-days); [Marina Times](https://marinatimesng.com/brief/nigeria-treasury-bills-market-review/); commercial lending 25–30% (2024–25) — [IJRISS 2026](https://rsisinternational.org/journals/ijriss/uploads/vol10-iss6-pg3334-3360-202606_pdf.pdf)
- Cost leakages borne by cooperatives: titling ₦3–10m and 12–24 months; infrastructure self-provision ₦20–55m/acre (+20–30% of project cost) — [IJRISS 2026](https://rsisinternational.org/journals/ijriss/uploads/vol10-iss6-pg3334-3360-202606_pdf.pdf)
- International resale-control models for contrast: Zurich members "never own the flat and cannot sell it on for a capital gain"; Vienna tenant down-payments depreciated 1%/yr (90% refunded after 10 years), rents track cost; Mitchell-Lama co-ops are limited equity ("can't sell them for a profit"); Sweden tenant-owner shares "traded at market value" since the 1970s — [housingcoop.eu Zurich](https://www.housingcoop.eu/cities/zurich); [housingcoop.eu Vienna](https://www.housingcoop.eu/cities/vienna); [GPLA Vienna overview](https://resources.gpla.co/vienna/overview); [Wikipedia Mitchell-Lama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitchell%E2%80%93Lama_Housing_Program); [CHI Sweden](https://www.housinginternational.coop/co-ops/sweden/)

### Inferences
- Nigerian cooperative estates behave like Sweden's market-resale model, not Zurich/Vienna/Mitchell-Lama: the society acts as a one-off bulk buyer/developer at or near cost, and the land appreciation is passed to members and captured on resale. Hence the society's "return" is mainly member welfare plus any cost-plus margin and fees, which no source quantifies.
- A cooperative lending members' savings at 10–20% versus investing in land: Nicon Town's ~18–25% nominal CAGR (2001–2022/26) beat that only because of exceptional location and the 2023–24 devaluation; FMBN-type estates are priced at cost and so generate little or no surplus for the society (by design, since finance is concessional and buyers are NHF contributors).
- Infrastructure and titling leakages (20–30% of cost, 1–2 years) are the main reason the ~₦33bn Lagos cooperative capital pool has produced little housing: the money is tied up in unserviced, untitled land.

### Gaps
- No Nigerian source quantifies cooperative estate margins, management fees, buy-back clauses or society-level surplus from estates; bye-laws of CEMCS/COOPLAG/NIMCOS were not found online.
- No data on whether societies retain any land/common assets (e.g., commercial plots) within estates.

## International cooperative-housing benchmarks

### Takeaway
Cooperative/limited-profit housing is 18–25% of stock in Sweden, Vienna, Zurich and ~10% in German cities, delivering 30–50% rent discounts through cost-rent and asset-lock rules; in Africa, Kenya's NACHU has facilitated ~30,000 units and Ethiopia's state-led IHDP ~383,000 condominium units, while Canada's co-ops are only 0.7% of dwellings.

### Cited Findings
- Sweden: cooperative (tenant-owner) housing 24% of stock, 1,212,299 units (2021), 1,100,238 households; HSB 670,000 members, 346,000 co-op units, 4,100 cooperatives (2021); Riksbyggen 1,750 cooperatives (2023); members finance 75–80% of construction cost; shares traded at market value since the 1970s — [Cooperative Housing International](https://www.housinginternational.coop/co-ops/sweden/); tenant-ownership "recently reached 25 percent" — [Housing Studies 2024](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02673037.2024.2326156)
- Germany/Austria: cooperatives >10% of Berlin stock (2011); unweighted average 10.4% in German cities, 20.0% in Austrian cities; Austria ~15% nationally — [Cambridge Social Science History](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/social-science-history/article/varieties-of-social-democracy-and-cooperativism-explaining-the-historical-divergence-between-housing-regimes-in-nordic-and-germanspeaking-countries/E03A34D2D99755D0FC1161CC0033553F) (via snippet)
- Vienna: municipal 20.5% (~220,000) + limited-profit/cooperative 17.8% (~221,000) = 36.5% of dwellings; rents €5.10 (municipal), €6.10 (limited-profit), €7.50 median, €13.70 new private (net cold €/m²/month); 78% of households eligible; cost-rent law 1979 — [housingcoop.eu Vienna](https://www.housingcoop.eu/cities/vienna); ~180,000 LPHA units (18–20%) and 1%/yr depreciation of tenant contribution — [GPLA](https://resources.gpla.co/vienna/overview)
- Zurich: cooperatives 18% of dwellings (~42,000 flats, ~110 cooperatives); non-profit total 24%; cooperative rent €16.28 vs €31.05 new private (≈48% lower); 2011 referendum target one-third non-profit by 2050 — [housingcoop.eu Zurich](https://www.housingcoop.eu/cities/zurich); Switzerland-wide cooperatives ~8% of rental sector, canton Zurich 14.3%, Basel 12.9% — [Univ. Bern](https://www.geography.unibe.ch/research/political_urbanism_and_sustainable_spatial_development/news_from_our_research_and_teaching/housing_cooperatives_a_sustainable_third_way_for_affordable_housing_provision/index_eng.html)
- Canada: >92,000 co-op units, ~0.7% of occupied dwellings; >82,000 in Ontario, Quebec, BC — [HillNotes, Jul 2024](https://hillnotes.ca/2024/07/22/co-operative-housing-in-canada/)
- US Mitchell-Lama: ~140,000 apartments built 1950s–1978, about half limited-equity co-ops; residents cannot sell for profit — [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitchell%E2%80%93Lama_Housing_Program); [CU4ML primer](https://cu4ml.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/ML-Basics_2017.pdf)
- Uruguay FUCVAM: >35,000 households in 730 mutual-aid cooperatives (est. 1970) — [GEO](https://geo.coop/articles/what-world-can-learn-uruguays-housing-co-ops)
- Kenya: NACHU (est. 1979, operational 1987) has facilitated >30,000 low-cost units; ~2,000 units pre-pandemic with four projects under construction; strategic target 125,000 units; 550 member cooperatives representing ≥250,000 members; loans up to 5x savings, 3–6 yr tenor, 20% deposit; national need 250,000/yr vs 50,000 supplied; NACHU discontinued a project when member contributions fell — [Nation](https://nation.africa/kenya/business/enterprise/housing-sacco-promises-to-deliver-125-000-affordable-housing-units--3433052); [CHI Kenya](https://www.housinginternational.coop/co-ops/kenya/); [Commercial Property Kenya](https://commercialpropertykenya.com/nachu-discontinues-its-housing-project-upon-member-contribution-fall/). Mwalimu National Sacco planned 871 units for KSh2.9bn (≈KSh3.3m/unit) — [Business Daily](https://www.businessdailyafrica.com/bd/markets/mwalimu-sacco-plans-sh2-9bn-housing-estate-2075478); Stima Sacco 1,500 units for KSh4bn on 100 acres, Kasarani; Stima Investment handed over Syokimau homes — [The Standard](https://www.standardmedia.co.ke/lifestyle/article/2000128431/sacco-hands-over-houses-in-stima-village-project)
- Ethiopia IHDP: 383,000 condominium units supplied 2006–2018 (earlier assessment: 171,000) via savings-based lottery allocation — [Housing Studies 2024](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/02673037.2024.2427668); [UN-Habitat/E&U](https://www.environmentandurbanization.org/condominium-housing-ethiopia-integrated-housing-development-programme)
- South Africa: housing co-ops emerged ~1996 (Gauteng, 2,000 Hillbrow/Joubert Park/Berea tenants); government-initiated alternative tenure for low-to-moderate income — [CHI South Africa](https://www.housinginternational.coop/co-ops/south-africa/); [PMG briefing](https://pmg.org.za/committee-meeting/11241/)

### Inferences
- The models that deliver durable affordability (Zurich, Vienna, Mitchell-Lama, FUCVAM) all combine cost-rent or limited-equity pricing with an asset lock and long-term public land/finance; Sweden shows market-resale co-ops become a wealth vehicle for insiders over time, which is the trajectory Nigeria's IOC estates have followed.
- Kenya's SACCO-led model is the closest analogue to Nigeria's workplace cooperatives (teachers', utility staff) and shows similar scale limits: tens of thousands of units over four decades against a 250,000/yr need.

### Gaps
- No single-year figure for Germany's national cooperative share (GdW data) was retrieved; Zurich/Vienna figures are from a secondary aggregator (housingcoop.eu) rather than city statistical offices.
- No South African unit totals for cooperative/social housing were found.
