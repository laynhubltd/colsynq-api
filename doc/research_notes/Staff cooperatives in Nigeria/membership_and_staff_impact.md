# Staff Cooperative Membership in Nigeria and Its Measured Effect on Employees and Employers

Scope note: "Staff cooperatives" here means employer-based (workplace common-bond) cooperative thrift & credit / multipurpose societies registered under the Nigerian Co-operative Societies Act or state cooperative laws, with contributions deducted at payroll source. Evidence quality is flagged throughout: (M) = measured/survey/regression evidence with stated sample; (D) = documentary (bye-laws, statutes, regulator reports); (A) = anecdote/press reporting.

## Key Question 1: Membership mechanics (eligibility, fees, savings, waiting periods, exit, death, set-off)

### Takeaway
Nigerian staff-cooperative bye-laws follow a near-standard template: payroll-deducted monthly savings (floors range from N5,000 to N20,000/month in the examples found), a non-refundable entrance fee (N2,000-N5,000), a 6-12 month contribution period before the first loan, loans capped at 2x-5x savings with two staff guarantors, and a statutory right of set-off of any debt against savings, shares, dividends and the deceased member's nominee payout (Co-operative Societies Act s.16 and s.18). Exit is by 1-3 months' notice and is blocked while the member owes money or stands surety.

### Cited Findings
**Statutory framework (D)**
- Nigerian Co-operative Societies Act s.16: a registered society "shall have a charge" on the shares, interests, deposits and any dividend/bonus of a present, past or deceased member for any debt due, and "may set off any sum credited or payable" to the member or estate against that debt. s.17 makes a member's shares/interest not liable to attachment or sale by a court for the member's other debts. s.18 governs transfer of a deceased member's interest to a nominee/heir/legal representative, and the society "shall pay all other moneys due to the deceased member" to them. — [Nigerian Co-operative Societies Act (FAO Lex copy)](https://faolex.fao.org/docs/pdf/nig120579.pdf)
- Labour Act s.5: an employer "may with the consent of a worker make deductions from the wages of the worker and pay to the appropriate person any contributions to provident or pension funds or other schemes agreed to by the worker and approved by the State Authority"; and "the total amount of deductions that may be made from the wages of a worker in any one month shall not exceed one-third of the wages of the worker for that month." — [Labour Act s.5 (jurist.ng)](https://jurist.ng/labour_act/sec-5)
- A Mondaq commentary on NICN case law (Asana v First Bank) notes the court held "the existence of the employment cannot operate as a condition precedent to the repayment of the personal loan" and that "the obligation to repay the staff loan survives the termination"; the court limited the lender's recovery to outstanding principal in that case because converting a concessional staff loan to commercial rates after constructive dismissal was "double jeopardy." (This concerns employer staff loans, not cooperative loans, but it is the closest NICN authority located on post-exit loan recovery.) — [Mondaq: Is a terminated/dismissed worker obliged to repay staff loans?](https://www.mondaq.com/nigeria/employment-litigation-tribunals/760262/is-a-terminateddismissed-worker-obliged-to-repay-staff-loans)

**Eligibility (D)**
- NFIU Staff Cooperative (2020 bye-law): membership open to serving staff and to "Retired or former staff of the NFIU proven to be of good character, provided such former or retired staff shall not vie for or hold any position in the MC." Seconded staff may borrow only with two guarantors who "must be confirmed staff and must have at least a minimum of five (5) years to serve in the Unit." An applicant who has left the Unit's service may be considered for a loan "below 70% of his contribution or savings" with two confirmed-staff guarantors having at least ten years to retirement. — [NFIUCOOP Bye-Law 2020](https://www.nfiu.gov.ng/NFIUCooperative/Files/NFIUCOOP%20BYE%20LAW.pdf)
- CBN Multipurpose Cooperative: credit eligibility requires "confirmed staff with 12+ months continuous contributions"; "Unconfirmed staff are ineligible." — [CBN Coop Credit & Thrift](https://www.thecbncoop.com/service/view/1)
- Accion MFB Staff Cooperative: has "Ordinary and Associate members"; an Associate Member (contract staff) "may not be granted credit facilities by the Society in excess of the member's net savings with the Society and repayment period shall not be more than the contract end date." Membership terminates on "Exit from AMfB payroll." — [Accion MFB Staff Cooperative Bye-Laws](http://accionmfbcoop.com.ng/assets/files/bye_law.pdf)
- Niger Delta University cooperative: member is "Ordinarily an employee of Niger Delta University. A member who leaves the employment of the University may retain his membership therein, but may not borrow there-from in excess of his Assets." Electoral Committee must ensure aspirants to the committee "are confirmed members of staff of the university." — [NDU Cooperative Bye-Laws](https://nducoop.com/uploads/Bye-Laws.pdf)
- ASUU (LASU Ojo) cooperative requires each new member to render "a true and complete statement of his indebtedness outside the society" on joining (a loan-stacking control). — [ASUU Ojo Cooperative Bye-Laws](https://asuuojocoop.com/downloads/bye-laws.pdf)

**Joining fees, shares and minimum savings (D)**
- NFIU: non-refundable application fee N2,000; re-admission fee N10,000 after a minimum 12-month gap; minimum ordinary monthly savings N20,000 "deducted from members' salaries at source"; savings split 60% "Savings account" (counts for loans) and 40% "Investment account" (does not); every member signs a letter of authority for salary deduction; shares at N1 each, no member may hold more than 20% of issued shares. — [NFIUCOOP Bye-Law 2020](https://www.nfiu.gov.ng/NFIUCooperative/Files/NFIUCOOP%20BYE%20LAW.pdf)
- Accion MFB: admission fee N2,000, then "subscribe to the required minimum shares and begin to make savings contributions." — [Accion MFB bye-laws](http://accionmfbcoop.com.ng/assets/files/bye_law.pdf)
- NDU: non-refundable entrance fee N5,000; Management Committee "may grant or refuse admission." — [NDU bye-laws](https://nducoop.com/uploads/Bye-Laws.pdf)
- CBN Coop: active members contribute N10,000/month; retired/junior staff N5,000/month. — [CBN Coop](https://www.thecbncoop.com/service/view/1)
- NLNG Staff Cooperative: members "pay a registration fee, subscribe to shares equally with all other members, and commit to a monthly minimum savings amount." — [NLNG Staff Cooperative - About](https://nlng.coop.ng/About)
- EFInA 2012 (community/trader cooperatives, not staff): average joining fee N1,434 across three states; N2,000-N4,000 in Enugu and Oyo; monthly contributions N2,000-N20,000 in Enugu. — [EFInA Understanding Co-operatives in Nigeria 2012 (Key Findings)](https://www.efina.org.ng/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/Understanding-Co-operatives-in-NigeriaKeyFindings.pdf)

**Waiting period, loan multiples, guarantors, interest (D)**
- NFIU: loan eligibility after "a contributing member for a period not less than Six (6) months"; maximum credit "Two (2) times the total contribution"; "Two (2) Guarantors, at least one of which must be a regular of the Unit"; general meeting fixes the loan interest rate "provided that this rate shall not exceed ten (10) per cent." Surplus distribution: 52% dividends, 25% reserve fund, 7% education, 7.5% honoraria, etc. — [NFIUCOOP Bye-Law 2020](https://www.nfiu.gov.ng/NFIUCooperative/Files/NFIUCOOP%20BYE%20LAW.pdf)
- CBN Coop: 12 months' contributions before the normal loan; normal loan up to 2x net savings, special and short-term loans up to 5x; 2020 rates 5.0% (normal/commodity), 12.5% (special), 11.75% (investment), 3.0-7.5% short-term; 12-month tenor for normal/special, 48 months for investment loans; savings "grow approximately 12% annually" with an annual bonus after each AGM. — [CBN Coop](https://www.thecbncoop.com/service/view/1)
- Niger Delta University: a member who leaves "may not borrow ... in excess of his Assets." — [NDU bye-laws](https://nducoop.com/uploads/Bye-Laws.pdf)

**Exit: resignation, transfer, dismissal, retirement, death (D)**
- NFIU: withdrawal "subject to one (1) Months' notice in writing ... provided that the withdrawing member is not in any form indebted to the Society"; an administrative charge "shall be charged on all refund to the withdrawing member"; a past member remains liable for society debts incurred within six months before cessation; where a member's employment is withdrawn, "entitlements in the Society, if any, or his/her guarantor shall be called upon to repay the outstanding loan"; share withdrawal may be limited so that "not more than (5%) of all applications to the withdrawal of shares are treated at once within the same financial year" without the Chief Registrar's permission; members may withdraw max 60% of savings once a year and only after all loans are repaid, and a surety cannot withdraw until the borrower has repaid at least 20%; investment-account funds are locked until year-end surplus/loss is declared; 6 consecutive months' non-contribution makes the account dormant/suspended. — [NFIUCOOP Bye-Law 2020](https://www.nfiu.gov.ng/NFIUCooperative/Files/NFIUCOOP%20BYE%20LAW.pdf)
- Accion MFB: termination by death, insanity, expulsion, "Consistent failure to make savings, to repay loans," or "Exit from AMfB payroll"; withdrawal on one month's notice "provided that the member withdrawing is neither in debt to the Society nor surety"; "Right of Set-off: Any money due on any account from this Society to a member, or a past member, shall be set off in payment of any sum which he owes or for which he stands Surety"; nominees receive "a sum representing the deceased member's share or interest at the time of his death." — [Accion MFB bye-laws](http://accionmfbcoop.com.ng/assets/files/bye_law.pdf)
- NDU: three months' notice to resign, and "no members shall resign until he or she has paid all sums due ... or where he is surety(ies) for the debt of another member(s) until that debt has been paid"; identical set-off clause; nominee "shall ... be paid the value of the Assets less any loan due." — [NDU bye-laws](https://nducoop.com/uploads/Bye-Laws.pdf)
- ASUU Ojo: on death/insanity/expulsion the society pays the nominee or legal representative "the sum of his credit less any sum due to the society"; re-admission only after 12 months. — [ASUU Ojo bye-laws](https://asuuojocoop.com/downloads/bye-laws.pdf)
- Search-result snippet from a university cooperative bye-law (NIFESAF ESUT): resignation takes effect on acceptance by the executives and the member "shall be eligible to withdraw his savings within a period of THREE months of the Executives' acceptance subject to a deduction of an administrative charge of N5,000." — [NIFESAF ESUT Bye-Laws](https://www.nemcos.org/files/shares/17_0323%20NIFESAF%20ESUT%20Cooperative%20%20BYE%20laws%20Submitted.pdf) (snippet only; full PDF not parsed)
- EFInA 2012 qualitative FGD quote (Kebbi, male 35-50): "We have our constitution book, all details are stated, it is impossible to leave the group voluntarily and demand for refund of money." EFInA also notes "Members also stand the risk of losing their savings if co-ops are mismanaged." — [EFInA Understanding Co-operatives Qualitative Report 2012](https://www.efina.org.ng/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/Understanding-Co-operatives-in-NigeriaQualitative-Report.pdf)
- EFInA 2010 A2F described cooperatives as "semi-formal and popular but minimally used despite their 'access to loan' mantra, with long procedures to join or exit and restrictive regulations." — [EFInA A2F 2010 Key Findings](https://efina.org.ng/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/A2F-2010-Final-Report.pdf) (as summarised in search results)

### Inferences
- The combination of statutory set-off (Act s.16), bye-law bans on exit while indebted or standing surety, and annual caps on share/savings withdrawals means a member's savings are effectively illiquid collateral; exit refunds are structurally slow even in well-run societies.
- Probationary/unconfirmed and contract staff are typically admitted to savings but excluded from, or capped in, borrowing (CBN: ineligible; Accion: loan capped at own savings and tenor to contract end).
- Loan multiples (2x-5x savings) with 6-12 month waits mean the first meaningful loan usually arrives in year two of membership.

### Gaps
- No systematic survey of staff-cooperative bye-laws exists; the parameters above come from five institutions (NFIU, CBN, Accion MFB, NDU, ASUU-LASU) and may not represent state civil service or oil & gas cooperatives.
- No NICN judgment specifically on cooperative loan set-off against gratuity/pension was retrievable (NICN portal link id=10996 returned 404; search results indicated a judgment involving university retirees and unremitted cooperative contributions but the text could not be fetched).
- Treatment on inter-ministry transfer (whether membership continues across MDAs under IPPIS) is not documented in sources found.

## Key Question 2: Participation (share of staff who join, member counts, balances, demographics)

### Takeaway
No national statistic on the share of employees who join staff cooperatives exists; the only institution-level figure found is FUTA Akure, where 272 of 297 surveyed staff (92%) were cooperative members (2009). Institutional sizes range from about 1,300 members with N23bn assets (NLNG) to about 2,900 members (Ambrose Alli University). National cooperative membership claims range from 1.4 million (2010 estimate cited by EFInA) to "over 30 million" (CFAN, 2023), and the two are not reconcilable.

### Cited Findings
- (M) FUTA Akure, 2009: 350 questionnaires administered (about 22% of staff), 297 retrieved (85%); "Two hundred and seventy-two (272) respondents indicated membership of the cooperative societies in the institution while the other twenty-five responses did not belong to any cooperative society." Eight registered cooperatives exist on campus (FUTA Housing Corporation, SSANU, Women Club, ASUU, Multi-purpose, God-favour, Equipment Maintenance Centre, Primary Staff). Male respondents were "over two times" female. 67% of respondents lived in rented accommodation, 20% in personal houses; HR data: 58% rented, 26% personal houses, 10% staff quarters. — [Adedeji & Olotuah, European Scientific Journal 8(12), 2012](https://eujournal.org/index.php/esj/article/download/181/185)
- (D) NLNG Staff Cooperative (Bonny): registered 28 Aug 2003; 1,315 members (Feb 2025); asset base over N23 billion (end-2024). — [NLNG Staff Cooperative - About](https://nlng.coop.ng/About)
- (M/D) Ambrose Alli University Workers/Farmers Multi-Purpose Cooperative Society, Edo State, established 1991, "membership strength of 2,924 members"; challenges "include theft/fraud and mismanagement." — [IJKCDT governance study](https://ijkcdt.org/index.php/ijkcdt/article/download/579/15) (abstract via search; full text not parsed)
- (D) Nigeria Police Cooperative Society (POLCOOP): established 1977 under Force Order No. 130; deductions were mandatory N1,000/month for the retired CSP from 1990-2006 before formal registration in 2007, then N5,000/month voluntary. — [FIJ, POLCOOP withholds retired CSP's savings](https://fij.ng/article/police-cooperative-society-withholds-retired-csps-savings-because-it-mishandled-his-records/); [SaharaReporters 2022](https://saharareporters.com/2022/11/10/police-officers-accuse-nigeria-police-cooperative-society-fraud-lament-6-months-delay)
- (D) CFAN Executive Secretary Emmanuel Atama (Apr 2023): "over 30 million membership bases across the 36 states in Nigeria and the FCT-Abuja"; four FCT departmental cooperatives "have consistently declared over N300 million surplus in the last 6 years." — [Nairametrics](https://nairametrics.com/2023/04/26/over-30-million-nigerians-belong-to-cooperative-societies/)
- (D) EFInA 2012: "In 2010, it was estimated that there were over 80,000 co-operative groups with over 1.4 million members in 605 local government areas in Nigeria. However, there is little systematic data available on the co-operative sector." — [EFInA 2012 Key Findings](https://www.efina.org.ng/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/Understanding-Co-operatives-in-NigeriaKeyFindings.pdf)
- (M) EFInA A2F 2010: "2.0 million adults (24.4%) have a personal loan from informal societies/savings clubs/cooperatives" (24.4% of those with a loan). — [EFInA A2F 2010](https://efina.org.ng/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/A2F-2010-Final-Report.pdf) (via search summary)
- (M) EFInA A2F 2023: 26,930 adults interviewed nationally; Statista's EFInA-derived series states "the share of people using cooperatives as a driver of informal financial services in Nigeria declined" in 2023 but the value is paywalled. — [EFInA A2F 2023 presentation](https://efina.org.ng/wp-content/uploads/2024/03/A2F-2023-Event-Day-Presentation-Version4-1.pdf); [Statista](https://www.statista.com/statistics/1454819/share-of-population-using-co-operatives-in-nigeria/)
- (M) EFInA 2012 member demographics (n=700, Enugu/Kebbi/Oyo; trader/farmer cooperatives, not staff): 52% female/48% male; 62% urban; ages 25-34 (36%) and 35-44 (33%) dominate; 81% married; 47% some secondary, 28% some tertiary education; Kebbi only 22% women because state law had barred women. Contribution frequency: 50% monthly, 38% weekly; average annual contribution N29,301 per member. — [EFInA 2012 Key Findings](https://www.efina.org.ng/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/Understanding-Co-operatives-in-NigeriaKeyFindings.pdf)
- (M) Covenant University and Lead City University cooperative study: "membership of cooperative societies cut across people in different age, sex, educational attainment, levels of income and number of households." — [Campbell et al., Journal of Current Research Advances](https://www.journalcra.com/article/cooperative-society-and-employees%E2%80%99-welfare)

### Inferences
- Where a workplace cooperative exists in a Nigerian public institution, participation appears to be the norm rather than the exception (FUTA ~92%), consistent with the "near-compulsory culture" hypothesis, but this rests on one 2009 sample.
- The 30 million figure is an apex-body claim without published methodology; the 1.4 million (2010) estimate referred to registered primary societies. Neither is a measured staff-cooperative participation rate.

### Gaps
- No data found on share of staff who join by sector (public service, universities, banks, oil & gas) or by grade level, gender or age within staff cooperatives.
- No published aggregate of staff-cooperative savings balances; only NLNG's N23bn asset base is public.
- EFInA A2F 2023 cooperative usage percentage not retrievable without paywall; EFInA A2F PDF text could not be parsed for the cooperative line.

## Key Question 3: Motivations for joining and not joining

### Takeaway
Across Nigerian cooperative surveys the dominant motive is loan access without collateral (62-63% of EFInA's 700 members), followed by forced savings and social support; for staff cooperatives the housing-loan and emergency-finance motives are emphasised. Reasons for not joining are poorly documented in Nigeria; Kenya's FinAccess shows affordability (54.5%) and eligibility as the main barriers.

### Cited Findings
- (M) EFInA 2012 (n=700): 436 (62%) named "access to loans and credit" as the main motivation; of these 54% were rural and 53% women. State pattern: Oyo 60% "access to loans"; Enugu 60% "desire to save regularly"; Kebbi 32% "love and kinship amongst members." Chart: access to loans 63%, love/kinship 44%, desire to save regularly 34%, commitment to members' businesses 24%, business opportunities 23%, business knowledge 22%, skills training 20%, family heritage 11%. Top benefits cited: easy access to loans 62%, low interest 37%, support during social events 36%, ability to save a lump sum 32%, quick response to loan applications 13%, flexible repayment 12%, subsidised goods 12%, no collateral 11%. — [EFInA 2012 Key Findings](https://www.efina.org.ng/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/Understanding-Co-operatives-in-NigeriaKeyFindings.pdf)
- (M) Akerele & Adekunmbi (2018), Ogun State thrift & credit members: 48.1% "enjoyed loan benefit," 40.7% "business improvement benefit," 9.3% "savings and dividend benefits." — [ResearchGate abstract](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/324952008_Impacts_of_Cooperative_Thrift_and_Credit_Facilities_on_Members'_Business_Output_in_Ogun_State_Nigeria) (abstract via search; full text 403)
- (M) Okafor et al. (2025), university workers in Aniocha North, Delta State (population 110, sample 86): membership "help university workers to access housings loans" and "to cope with financial emergencies, such as illness and accidents." — [IJMRGE 6(1) 2025](https://www.allmultidisciplinaryjournal.com/uploads/archives/20250101175012_F-24-252.1.pdf)
- (M) Covenant/Lead City study: members "reported satisfaction with cooperative models for meeting welfare needs"; loan administration "was also found commendable by members." — [Campbell et al.](https://www.journalcra.com/article/cooperative-society-and-employees%E2%80%99-welfare)
- (D) Cooperatives' own marketing emphasises collateral-free credit and housing: ASUU Ojo objects include "To secure wholesale mortgage, either for on-lending to members or for housing development" and direct loans "as obtainable in the Civil Service, e.g. Car Loan, Compassionate Loan." — [ASUU Ojo bye-laws](https://asuuojocoop.com/downloads/bye-laws.pdf)
- (A) Daily Trust (Jul 2026) Abuja civil servants: one GL14 officer with 24 years' service used cooperative loans and "Successfully funded three children's education"; another "Attempted poultry and fish farming using cooperative loan" and lost money. — [Daily Trust](https://dailytrust.com/high-cost-of-living-pushes-abuja-civil-servants-into-debt/)
- (M, Kenya comparator) FinAccess 2024 SACCO report: affordability is the top barrier to use, "cited by 54.5 percent of non-users," more prevalent among women and older adults; "eligibility is a greater barrier for younger, unemployed individuals"; lack of awareness 22.2%; trust concerns 6.2%. Reasons for stopping: voluntary withdrawal 51.7%, inability to afford services 46.2%. — [FSD Kenya SACCO Sub-Sector Report, Nov 2025](https://www.fsdkenya.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/SACCO-Subsector-Report-Lessons-from-FinAccess-Surveys.pdf)
- (A) EFInA 2010 A2F characterised cooperatives as "minimally used despite their 'access to loan' mantra, with long procedures to join or exit." — [EFInA A2F 2010](https://efina.org.ng/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/A2F-2010-Final-Report.pdf)

### Inferences
- In a workplace setting the "forced savings" motive is structurally reinforced: payroll deduction and bye-law lock-ins (no withdrawal while indebted, 60% annual cap at NFIU) make the cooperative a commitment device, which members value even when returns are modest.
- Non-joining is most plausibly driven by inability to spare the monthly minimum (N10,000-N20,000 at CBN/NFIU is a large share of junior-grade pay) and, for contract/probationary staff, by eligibility rules.

### Gaps
- No Nigerian survey of non-members' reasons for not joining a staff cooperative was found.
- No staff-cooperative-specific ranking of motives (loan vs housing vs consumer goods vs social) with sample sizes was found; EFInA's motive data covers trader/farmer cooperatives.

## Key Question 4: Measured effects on members (savings, credit, assets, housing, business, school fees, welfare)

### Takeaway
Nigerian studies consistently report positive associations between staff-cooperative membership and savings, credit access, housing and welfare, but they are small cross-sectional member-only surveys (n = 86 to 683) using Likert scales, chi-square or simple regression without non-member controls, so effect sizes are not causal. The strongest quantitative Nigerian evidence is from rural/trader cooperatives (income 10-28% higher for members); the housing literature shows cooperative channels deliver for a minority of subscribers (24-43%).

### Cited Findings
**Staff/university cooperative studies (M)**
- Okafor, Udemadu, Braimah & Ifesinachi (2025), five universities' staff in Aniocha North LGA, Delta State; population 110, Taro Yamane sample 86; Likert questionnaire, means and regression. Finding: "university workers who are members of thrift and credit society have higher savings and access to credit"; membership helps "access housings loans, which improve the living standard"; grand mean 4.2 on savings items "indicate that savings of university workers have increased." No comparison group. — [IJMRGE 6(1):51-60](https://www.allmultidisciplinaryjournal.com/uploads/archives/20250101175012_F-24-252.1.pdf)
- Ajayi & Chilokwu (2021), universities in Ekiti State, n=355, multiple regression: "access to educational loans, land acquisition, and household asset purchases significantly improved staff welfare." — cited in [IJSBER 13(1) 2025](https://eajournals.org/ijsber/wp-content/uploads/sites/85/2025/01/Effect-of-Cooperative-Thrift.pdf) (original: British Journal of Management and Marketing Studies 4(4):21-33; not fetched)
- Olujimi et al. (2021), Akure: data from 683 house owners; "pooled member contributions and salary deductions funded housing loans, while chi-square tests indicated that lending criteria did not significantly hinder members from accessing housing loans." — cited in [IJSBER 13(1) 2025](https://eajournals.org/ijsber/wp-content/uploads/sites/85/2025/01/Effect-of-Cooperative-Thrift.pdf)
- Campbell, Olurinola, Oluwatobi & Taliat, Covenant University and Lead City University cooperatives: Likert 5-point survey, purposive sampling, descriptive and inferential analysis; members satisfied that cooperatives meet welfare needs; sample size not given in abstract. — [Journal of Current Research Advances](https://www.journalcra.com/article/cooperative-society-and-employees%E2%80%99-welfare)
- Adedeji & Olotuah (2012), FUTA Akure (n=297): of eight campus cooperatives, "Four of the cooperative societies render home ownership schemes; two ... rental loan schemes while the other two ... fund several property development schemes"; conclusion: "the level of accessibility of low-income earners to housing finance in Nigeria is still very low despite the intermediation of private developers and cooperative societies." — [ESJ 8(12)](https://eujournal.org/index.php/esj/article/download/181/185)
- Lagos State institution-based cooperatives housing study: members reporting their subscription had been delivered "fell below average for majority of the channels such as land acquisition (38.2%), housing development (23.8%) and bulk material procurement (43.3%)." — [ResearchGate: Success Rate of Co-operative Societies' Housing Provision in Lagos](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/319321364_Empirical_Assessment_of_Success_Rate_of_Co-operative_Societies'_Housing_Provision_in_Lagos_State_Nigeria) (abstract via search; full text 403)

**Thrift & credit society studies, mixed membership (M)**
- IJSBER (2025), Awka South LGA, Anambra: 10 functional thrift & credit societies, population 486; simple regressions: credit disbursement coefficient 11.66 (p<0.05) and savings mobilisation coefficient 1.61 (p<0.05) on members' income level; financial education not significant (p>0.05). — [IJSBER 13(1):1-17](https://eajournals.org/ijsber/wp-content/uploads/sites/85/2025/01/Effect-of-Cooperative-Thrift.pdf)
- Aribaba et al. (2018), Ondo State small businesses: "cooperative loans explained 52% of employment growth, with a 30% increase in employment linked to loan availability from cooperatives." — cited in [IJSBER 13(1) 2025](https://eajournals.org/ijsber/wp-content/uploads/sites/85/2025/01/Effect-of-Cooperative-Thrift.pdf)
- EFInA 2012 (n=700 members, 150 managers): 44.9% had applied for a loan; 58.6% of applicants sought more than N40,000; typical loan N50,000, max N1.2m; average loan size N33,236; disbursement lag 1-2 weeks (over a month in rural areas); repayment 10-12 months; 42% of borrowers had an outstanding balance (N40,000-N60,000). Members' stated loan use: business stock 61%, personal/family emergency 24%, buy land/build house 8%, agricultural inputs 7%, setting up a business 6%. — [EFInA 2012 Key Findings](https://www.efina.org.ng/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/Understanding-Co-operatives-in-NigeriaKeyFindings.pdf)
- Southwest Nigeria rural cooperative members' income "approximately 10% higher" than non-members. — [ResearchGate: Impact of Cooperative Membership on Rural Income Generation](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/351705321_Impact_of_Cooperative_Membership_on_Rural_Income_Generation_in_Southwest_Nigeria) (abstract via search)
- Anambra farmer-members: "cooperative members are able to generate more income than non-cooperative members by 28% per year." — [ResearchGate: Effects of Membership of Cooperative Organisations, Anambra](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/282325374_Effects_Of_Membership_Of_Cooperative_Organisations_And_Determinants_On_Farmer-_Members'_Income_In_Rural_Anambra_State_Nigeria) (abstract via search)
- Rural Nigeria household panel: cooperative membership and extension access studied for effects on "technology adoption, asset ownership and poverty." — [PMC5614096](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5614096/) (agricultural cooperatives; not staff)

**Kenya comparator (M)**
- FinAccess 2024 SACCO report: SACCO loan purposes "education (34.2 percent) and land/housing (26.7 percent)"; "urban members and males receiving larger loans"; "the average health of a SACCO member is generally better than that of the average Kenyan. This could be linked to the employment status of SACCO members." — [FSD Kenya SACCO Sub-Sector Report](https://www.fsdkenya.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/SACCO-Subsector-Report-Lessons-from-FinAccess-Surveys.pdf)
- Umoja Wendani SACCO case: "dividends, credit facilities, and interest rates positively influence financial empowerment in SACCO members." — [Academia.edu](https://www.academia.edu/43552853/Influence_of_Savings_and_Credit_Cooperative_Societies_Saccos_Products_and_Services_on_the_Financial_Empowerment_of_the_Members_in_Kenya_A_Case_of_Umoja_Wendani_Sacco) (abstract via search)

### Inferences
- The Nigerian staff-cooperative literature is unanimous in direction but weak in design: member-only samples, self-reported Likert outcomes, no counterfactual. Selection (better-off staff join and stay) cannot be ruled out, and the "higher savings" finding is partly tautological because savings are compulsory for members.
- Housing is the most-cited aspirational benefit but the only delivery-rate data (Lagos: 24-43% of subscribers served; FUTA: 20% in own homes) suggest cooperatives are a partial, slow channel to home ownership.

### Gaps
- No Nigerian study with a matched non-member control group, difference-in-differences, or administrative payroll data for staff cooperatives was found.
- No study quantifying asset acquisition (vehicles, land) attributable to staff-cooperative loans with effect sizes.
- Full texts of the Ogun State (Akerele & Adekunmbi), Lagos housing and Ambrose Alli studies could not be retrieved (ResearchGate 403).

## Key Question 5: Effects on employers (retention, morale, productivity, salary-advance demand, costs and risks)

### Takeaway
No Nigerian study measuring staff-cooperative effects on retention, absenteeism or productivity was found; the employer-side case rests on HR commentary that concessional staff credit "builds loyalty." The employer's documented costs are concrete: payroll administration of deductions, legal exposure for deducting but not remitting (Oyo Water Corporation ~N200m strike; IPPIS withholding N4.4m from an NBS staff cooperative for 3 years), and reputational damage when an in-house cooperative fails (NNPC, Police).

### Cited Findings
- (A/HR) AnooreHR payroll guide: "Handled well, a salary advance or staff loan is a low-cost benefit that builds loyalty"; deductions require written consent "in line with the Labour Act's limits on deductions." — [AnooreHR](https://anoorehr.com/blog/salary-advances-staff-loans-payroll-nigeria)
- (A/HR) Ribi People 2026 benefits guide: "Soft loans and salary advances ... help employees manage financial shocks and are highly valued benefits in the current high-interest market." — [Ribi People](https://ribipeople.com/resources/employee-benefits-in-nigeria-the-complete-2026-guide)
- (D) Employer's remittance duty under Labour Act s.5: employer may "make deductions ... and pay to the appropriate person." — [Labour Act s.5](https://jurist.ng/labour_act/sec-5)
- (A) Oyo State Water Corporation staff (AUPCTRE) locked the state Secretariat gates (Feb 2021) over unremitted cooperative deductions: N122m owed as of September, "over N200 million" by January, covering "Cooperative contributions, Check-up bonus, Leave bonus, Car loan deduction, Housing loan deduction"; corporation chairman said payments had been made and "only one cooperative society reported non-receipt." — [Tribune Online](https://tribuneonlineng.com/oyo-workers-protest-govt-failure-to-remit-n200-million-cooperative-deductions/); follow-on Oyo protests 2023 — [Vanguard](https://www.vanguardngr.com/2023/07/update-oyo-workers-barricade-govt-secretariat-over-deductions-palliatives-allowances/)
- (A) IPPIS deducted N4.39m (also reported N4.42m) from NBS Kwara Staff Multipurpose Cooperative members in Dec 2021 and had not remitted it by Nov 2024; "Some of the members who needed loans to service some necessities in their daily lives could no longer access it"; some affected members had retired or died; the cooperative has "neither an email nor a phone number" for IPPIS. — [FIJ](https://fij.ng/article/ippis-withholds-nbs-kwara-staff-cooperatives-n4m-since-2022/)
- (A) IPPIS also reported "wrongfully deducting loans from workers' salaries but not remitting them" and duplicate deductions. — [FIJ](https://fij.ng/article/ippis-wrongfully-deducting-loans-from-workers-salaries-but-not-remitting-them/)
- (A) Lenders' association says N12bn deducted from over 2,400 federal civil servants' pay via IPPIS remained unremitted to banks/MFBs/finance companies as of Dec 2024, despite a 2023 Service Level Agreement with OAGF. — [Vanguard](https://www.vanguardngr.com/2024/12/remit-n12bn-loans-deducted-from-workers-salaries-creditors-urge-fg/)
- (A) Niger State civil servants protested May 2025 deductions (CPS rate raised 7.5% to 10% plus "Cooperative society repayments" and "Sharp Sharp loans") after a 17 Mar 2025 Head of Service circular resuming third-party deductions; a payroll error wrongly deducted Defined Benefit staff. — [SaharaReporters](https://saharareporters.com/2025/05/28/niger-state-workers-lament-10-deductions-may-salary-third-party-charges)
- (A) Kano State suspended loan deductions for three months (Apr 2025) while verifying debts, after finding 21,000 of ~61,000 public servants indebted to private loan firms. — [BusinessDay](https://businessday.ng/news/article/21000-kano-civil-servants-indebted-to-private-loan-firms/)
- (A) Federal Government Staff Housing Loans Board: "Heavy loan deductions render workers ineligible for housing loans"; ceiling raised to N20m "but few can access funds" (Executive Secretary Hajiya Salamatu Ahmed). — [Daily Trust](https://dailytrust.com/high-cost-of-living-pushes-abuja-civil-servants-into-debt/)
- (M, Kenya comparator) SASRA 2024 report: employer-withheld SACCO deductions KSh3.49bn in 2024 (up from KSh2.59bn in 2023), affecting 85 SACCOs and 55,600+ members; 74.5% of withheld sums were loan repayments; counties held 46%. SASRA 2025 report: KSh3.92bn, 104,000+ members. — [Serrari Group](https://serrarigroup.com/saccos-confront-ksh-3-5-billion-in-unremitted-deductions-raising-liquidity-and-trust-concerns/); [Kenya Times](https://thekenyatimes.com/business/counties-universities-and-govt-firms-hold-back-billions-deducted-from-workers-salaries/)
- (M, Kenya) Employer-based regulated SACCOs held 64.49% of SACCO industry assets in 2024; salary deduction is the repayment channel for 59.73% of borrowers (up from 46.03% in 2021). — [FSD Kenya SACCO report](https://www.fsdkenya.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/SACCO-Subsector-Report-Lessons-from-FinAccess-Surveys.pdf)

### Inferences
- Employers (especially state governments and MDAs on IPPIS) are the weak link in the deduction chain; when they hold back remittances, members lose loan access and cooperatives face liquidity stress, converting an HR benefit into an industrial-relations liability.
- Nigeria has no published equivalent of SASRA's non-remittance tally, so the scale of employer default to staff cooperatives is unknown but the Kenyan data (billions of shillings, mostly public employers) suggest it is systemic in comparable payroll-deduction systems.

### Gaps
- No Nigerian quantitative evidence on retention, absenteeism, morale or productivity attributable to staff cooperatives; the Nigerian retention literature (universities, SMEs) does not isolate cooperatives as a variable.
- No evidence on whether cooperatives reduce formal salary-advance requests to employers.
- No published Nigerian regulator data on employer non-remittance to cooperatives.

## Key Question 6: Downsides and controversies (over-indebtedness, loan stacking, unremitted deductions, executive fraud, retirement set-off, pensioners)

### Takeaway
Press and FIJ investigations document a recurring pattern: (i) over-deduction leaving civil servants with little take-home pay despite the Labour Act's one-third cap (Kano: 21,000 of 61,000 staff indebted to private lenders; Abuja GL14 officer "I earn, but I hardly see my money"); (ii) employers/IPPIS deducting but not remitting; (iii) executive fraud or illiquidity in large staff cooperatives (NNPC Lagos: N806m loss, six-year withdrawal freeze; POLCOOP: 6-12 month delays, lost pre-2019 records; NLNG cooperative defrauded of N700m by an outsider). Nigeria's measured evidence on over-indebtedness is limited to the Kano count; Kenya's FinAccess found 75.1% of SACCO members who lost money blamed internal fraud.

### Cited Findings
**Over-indebtedness and "salary slave" dynamics**
- (D) Labour Act s.5 caps total monthly deductions at one-third of wages. — [Labour Act s.5](https://jurist.ng/labour_act/sec-5)
- (M/A) Kano SSG Umar Faruk (29 Apr 2025): "Over 21,000 of approximately 61,000 public servants in Kano State are indebted to private loan companies"; "Many workers, particularly educators, receive minimal or no take-home pay after loan deductions"; state suspended loan deductions for three months and found 240 double-salary and 217 duplicate-BVN cases in March. — [BusinessDay](https://businessday.ng/news/article/21000-kano-civil-servants-indebted-to-private-loan-firms/)
- (A) Daily Trust (1 Jul 2026): GL14 officer Gabriel Ajogun, 24 years in service, rents, no car: "I earn, but I hardly see my money. Loans finish everything"; "Multiple cooperative deductions and loan repayments under IPPIS consume salary"; development economist Dr Ibrahim Yusuf urged "reduced reliance on salary-linked lending"; financial expert Dr Chika Nwosu advised avoiding "overlapping loans from multiple sources." — [Daily Trust](https://dailytrust.com/high-cost-of-living-pushes-abuja-civil-servants-into-debt/)
- (A) Leadership: "civil servants watching deductions swallow their pay and some borrowing from one lender just to pay another"; civil servant Peter Ugbeche borrowed from cooperative societies for household expenses. — [Leadership](https://leadership.ng/nigerians-lament-debt-trap-as-borrowing-for-food-rent-soars/)
- (D) Payday/IPPIS lenders link loans to the employee's IPPIS number so repayment "is automatically deducted at source on payday and remitted to the lender before the remaining salary balance reaches the employee's account." — [Credit Direct guide](https://www.creditdirect.ng/blog/payday-loans-nigeria)
- (A) FIJ: IPPIS "deducted the exact amount ... twice" and deducted for "loan obligations they know nothing about." — [FIJ, WACS loan](https://fij.ng/article/through-ippis-wacs-loan-debiting-nigerian-workers-for-unknown-reasons/); [FIJ, mysterious loan](https://fij.ng/article/ippis-deducts-civil-servants-salaries-for-mysterious-loan-repayment/)
- (D) Cooperative control on stacking: ASUU Ojo bye-law requires a "true and complete statement of his indebtedness outside the society" at admission. — [ASUU Ojo bye-laws](https://asuuojocoop.com/downloads/bye-laws.pdf)
- (M) EFInA 2012 managers: "Members often do not pay back their loans on time and this depletes the cash"; 42% of borrowers had outstanding balances. — [EFInA 2012](https://www.efina.org.ng/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/Understanding-Co-operatives-in-NigeriaKeyFindings.pdf)
- (M) EKSU study: "Most loans disbursed by cooperative societies eventually become non-performing loans, with loan management procedures appearing poor." — [EKSU BF Journal](https://bfjournal.eksu.edu.ng/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/loan-default.pdf) (via search summary)

**Retirement set-off and pensioner complaints**
- (D) Statutory basis for set-off at exit/death is Act s.16; bye-laws apply it to "a member, or a past member." — [Co-operative Societies Act](https://faolex.fao.org/docs/pdf/nig120579.pdf); [Accion MFB bye-laws](http://accionmfbcoop.com.ng/assets/files/bye_law.pdf)
- (A) Search results referenced an NICN judgment in which university retirees claimed cooperative contributions deducted from salary were never remitted and sought payment at retirement; the judgment page could not be fetched. — [NICN judgment portal id=10996](https://nicnadr.gov.ng/judgment/judgement.php?id=10996) (404 on fetch; content unverified)
- (A) NNPC retirees report "No dividends received for approximately 10 years" alongside the six-year withdrawal freeze. — [Vanguard](https://www.vanguardngr.com/2025/05/nnpc-retirees-cry-out-over-possible-loss-of-savings-with-staff-cooperative/)

### Inferences
- The one-third cap in Labour Act s.5 is not operationally enforced across multiple lenders: IPPIS and state payrolls process cooperative, MFB and fintech deductions independently, so aggregate deductions can approach the full salary. Kano's three-month suspension is the only documented state-level corrective.
- Retirees are the most exposed members: they lose the payroll link, their savings are subject to set-off, and in weakly governed societies (NNPC, POLCOOP) withdrawal becomes a multi-year dispute. Record-keeping failures (POLCOOP pre-2019) compound this.
- Staff cooperatives in large parastatals/forces accumulate balances (NLNG N23bn) that make them attractive targets for both insider misappropriation and external land/investment fraud.

### Gaps
- No FCCPC action on staff cooperatives was found (FCCPC enforcement found in searches concerns digital lenders, not cooperatives).
- No NICN judgment text on cooperative loan set-off at retirement or on unremitted cooperative deductions could be retrieved; existence is indicated by search snippets only.
- No Nigerian survey measuring the share of staff-cooperative members with deductions above one-third of pay, or holding loans from multiple cooperatives/lenders simultaneously.

## Key Question 7: Comparative evidence (Kenya SACCOs, Ghana credit unions)

### Takeaway
Kenya's regulated SACCO system offers the best-measured comparator: 11.7% of adults used SACCOs in 2024 (3.3m adult members; 7.39m total members; KSh1.08trn assets), membership skews to formally employed and tertiary-educated adults, 60% repay via employer salary deduction, loans go mainly to education and land/housing, and employer non-remittance (KSh3.9bn in 2025) and internal fraud are the principal consumer-protection failures. Ghana evidence on workplace credit unions was not retrievable beyond CUA's acknowledgement that workplace credit unions exist.

### Cited Findings
- (M) FinAccess 2024 (CBK/KNBS/FSD Kenya): SACCO usage rose from 9.6% (2021) to 11.7% (2024); "Membership is highest among those with post-tertiary education and individuals in formal employment"; SACCOs served 3.3m adult members in 2024 vs 2.6m in 2021; monthly engagement 74.9% vs banks 58.7%; tertiary-educated usage 26.0% vs secondary 11.2%, primary 7.8%; gender gap widened from 0.3 to 3.7 points; "most Kenyans who access SACCOs are employed at 33.7 percent as there are more SACCOs related to formal employment"; mobile/digital SACCO usage rose from 19.08% to 55%. — [FSD Kenya SACCO Sub-Sector Report, Nov 2025](https://www.fsdkenya.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/SACCO-Subsector-Report-Lessons-from-FinAccess-Surveys.pdf); [Sacco Review](https://saccoreview.co.ke/sacco-usage-rises-from-9-6-in-2021-to-11-7-in-2024-finaccess-surveys/)
- (M) Loan purposes: education 34.2%, land/housing 26.7%; "Guarantors are the most common form of collateral, and direct salary deductions are the primary loan repayment method" (59.73% in 2024, up from 46.03% in 2021); the number of members accessing credit has declined since 2019 while average loan value rose; urban and male members get larger loans. — [FSD Kenya SACCO report](https://www.fsdkenya.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/SACCO-Subsector-Report-Lessons-from-FinAccess-Surveys.pdf)
- (M) SASRA: 356 regulated SACCOs, total assets KSh1.08trn (+10.72%), membership 7.39m (+7.94%) at end-2024; employer-based regulated SACCOs held 64.49% of industry assets; sector employed 12,005 people. — [FSD Kenya SACCO report](https://www.fsdkenya.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/SACCO-Subsector-Report-Lessons-from-FinAccess-Surveys.pdf)
- (M) Employer non-remittance: KSh3.49bn (2024), 85 SACCOs, 55,600 members, 74.5% loan repayments, counties 46%; KSh3.92bn (2025), 104,000 members, counties and public universities leading. — [Serrari Group](https://serrarigroup.com/saccos-confront-ksh-3-5-billion-in-unremitted-deductions-raising-liquidity-and-trust-concerns/); [Capital FM](https://capitalfm.africa/counties-public-universities-lead-in-sh3-92bn-unremitted-sacco-deductions/); Treasury plan to route SACCO deductions directly from payroll, bypassing employers — [Sacco Review](https://saccoreview.co.ke/sacco-deductions-to-skip-employers-in-treasury-payroll-plan/)
- (M) Welfare: SACCO member health "generally better than that of the average Kenyan," attributed to employment status; Uganda Koch Goma SACCO study reports improved "household savings, education affordability, and food security." — [FSD Kenya](https://www.fsdkenya.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/SACCO-Subsector-Report-Lessons-from-FinAccess-Surveys.pdf); [Academia.edu, Koch Goma](https://www.academia.edu/121364048/The_Role_of_Savings_and_Credit_Cooperatives_Towards_Improvement_of_Household_Welfare_in_Nwoya_District_A_case_study_of_Koch_Goma_SACCO)
- (D) Ghana: CUA is the apex of all cooperative credit unions; its Tema Chapter "includes community, parish, and workplace types of Credit Unions"; credit unions employ 3,142 people. — [CUA Credit Unions Overview](https://www.cua.org.gh/credit-unions/overview/); [CUA Tema Chapter](https://cua.org.gh/our-chapters/tema-chapter/)

### Inferences
- The Kenyan pattern (formal employees dominate membership, payroll deduction dominates repayment, public employers dominate non-remittance) mirrors the Nigerian anecdotes, but Kenya's SASRA supervision produces annual quantified exposure data that Nigeria's state-level cooperative registrars do not.
- Kenya's move to route SACCO deductions directly through Treasury payroll is a policy response Nigeria's IPPIS could in principle replicate, though IPPIS itself has been the non-remitting party in Nigerian cases.

### Gaps
- No Ghana workplace credit union outcome study retrieved (ILO IFLIP paper fetched but unreadable; University of Ghana thesis not fetched).
- WOCCU-specific studies of employer-based credit unions in Africa were not located within the tool budget.
