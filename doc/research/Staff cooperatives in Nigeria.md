# Payroll Check-Off Powers Nigeria's Staff Cooperatives

A Nigerian staff cooperative is a state-registered thrift-and-credit (usually "multipurpose") society whose common bond is one employer's payroll: each member signs a deduction authority, the employer deducts a fixed monthly saving and any loan instalment at source and remits it to the society, and the society lends the pool back to members at two to three times their savings, secured by two colleague-guarantors and the payroll itself rather than by collateral. It is a body corporate under the Nigerian Co-operative Societies Act (Cap. N98) registered by a State Director of Cooperatives, exempt from companies income tax and stamp duty, bound to put at least a quarter of each year's surplus into an indivisible reserve, and supervised administratively (audit, inquiry, arbitration, surcharge, dissolution) rather than prudentially. The model works remarkably well where the payroll is reliable and the committee is honest: oil-sector societies with 1,300–3,000 members have built **₦23–66 billion balance sheets** and delivered Lekki and Ikoyi estates whose plots appreciated more than twentyfold, while ordinary parastatal societies pay out 50–69% of surpluses as cash dividends every year. Its failure modes are equally specific: employers and the federal IPPIS payroll deducting but not remitting (₦112 million awarded against one local government with 10% interest), governance paralysis that froze **4,000+ NNPC members' savings for six years**, and liquidity crises when thrift money is sunk into property. The sector's size is unknown in any audited sense — the federal government's "370,000 societies, 30 million members, ₦1.7 trillion" is a ministerial estimate, and Nigeria does not appear in WOCCU's country tables at all — but on any reading it is roughly one-eighth of Kenya's SASRA-regulated SACCO system in dollar assets despite four times the population. The measured evidence on member welfare is unanimous in direction (higher savings, credit access, some housing) and weak in design (member-only Likert surveys, no counterfactual), so the strongest analytical conclusion is structural: a staff cooperative is a governance product wrapped around a payroll, and its value to members and employers is decided by remittance discipline, term-limited audited committees and a hard cap on illiquid assets, not by loan pricing.

This report builds on the earlier "Pooled capital business structures" report, which covers cooperatives as one of Nigeria's eight legal wrappers (one-member-one-vote, the 20% shareholding cap, the ICA surplus logic, tax treatment under the 2026 reforms and generic committee governance). Those points are referenced here only where the staff-cooperative context changes them.

## Registration takes three months in Lagos and three years in Abuja

The federal Act sets a low floor and leaves everything that matters to the states. A primary society may register with **at least ten persons** qualified for membership (six for an industrial society, five registered societies for a secondary), its name must contain "Co-operative" and end in "Limited", the statutory fee is "₦100 or such other sum as may be prescribed by the Minister or Commissioner", and the Director "shall within 60 days dispose of an application" ([Act ss.3–5, FAOLEX](https://faolex.fao.org/docs/pdf/nig120579.pdf)). Registration confers perpetual succession, a common seal, the capacity to hold property and sue, and exemption from stamp duties, instrument-registration fees and companies income tax (ss.6, 20). Because s.1 lets each Governor appoint a State Director and confer the Act's powers by Gazette, registration is in practice a state function — Lagos through the Department of Cooperative Services in the Ministry of Commerce, Cooperatives, Trade and Investment, the FCT through the Chief Registrar of Co-operatives in the FCTA — while the Federal Department of Cooperatives (now in the Ministry of Agriculture and Food Security) sets policy. The NFIU staff cooperative's 2020 bye-laws, certified "registered under Section 5 of the Nigerian Co-operatives Society Act" and "approved under the seal of the Chief Register of Co-operatives, Federal Capital Territory Administration", show the FCT registering a staff society directly under the federal Act ([NFIUCOOP Bye-Law 2020](https://www.nfiu.gov.ng/NFIUCooperative/Files/NFIUCOOP%20BYE%20LAW.pdf)).

State practice diverges sharply, and a staff cooperative's time-to-certificate depends on where its secretariat sits. Lagos, under its own Cooperative Societies Law signed on 7 February 2022, requires **20 founding members aged 18+**, a shared common bond, a verifiable secretariat address, a formal application to the Permanent Secretary, an inaugural meeting witnessed by a Ministry officer, payment of registration and trustee-training fees, induction of the President, Secretary and Treasurer at the Lagos State Cooperative College, purchase of statutory record books, a **three-month monitoring period**, and only then the certificate and registered bye-laws ([Lagos MCCTI](https://mccti.lagosstate.gov.ng/departments/cooperatives); [Mondaq, May 2023](https://www.mondaq.com/nigeria/corporate-and-company-law/1314598/registering-a-cooperative-society-in-lagos-state); [Pavestones Legal](https://pavestoneslegal.com/registering-a-cooperative-society-in-lagos-state/)). The FCT runs the older federal pattern: application signed by at least ten eligible persons with a certified formation resolution, feasibility study, four copies of proposed bye-laws, letters of intent and trustee passport photographs, a **₦25,000 processing fee** (plus ₦150,000–₦250,000 if a law firm is used), then a **Recognition Letter valid for three years** before a full Certificate of Registration ([ICA Legal, Dec 2025](https://ica.ng/how-to-register-a-co-operative-society-in-abuja-fct-requirements-costs-and-procedure/)). A widely repeated national schedule gives type-specific minimums — 50 for agricultural, 20 for savings and credit, 10 for others — followed by the same three-year recognition letter ([Legal500 / Fred-Young & Evans](https://www.legal500.com/intelligence/nigeria/strategy/procedure-for-registration-of-cooperative-society-in-nigeria)). No state publishes an official fee schedule; a claimed Lagos minimum of ₦130,000 appears only in an untraceable search snippet and should not be budgeted on.

Two post-registration realities matter for an accountant. First, registration is not permanent in practice: the FCT has run a recertification exercise since 2018, citing societies "operating without authorisation", poor financial records, officials overstaying tenure and "maladministration and sharp practices", with de-registration and EFCC referral threatened for non-compliance ([Abuja Digest, June 2022](http://abujadigest.info/2022/06/why-all-cooperative-societies-in-fct-must-be-recertified-secretary/)), and Lagos revalidated 2,359 societies and approved 807 annual accounts in a single year ([Nairametrics, May 2021](https://nairametrics.com/2021/05/18/co-operative-service-capital-base-in-lagos-rises-to-n33-billion-commissioner/)). Second, the 2025–2030 Renewed Hope Cooperative Reform and Revamp Programme adds a federal digital layer on top of state registration: a National Cooperative Societies Register, a Cooperative Verification Number for each society and a CoopID for each member, built by Seamfix under an MoU signed on 12 August 2026 at no cost to governments, alongside a review of the Act and a proposed Cooperative Bank of Nigeria ([FMINO, Oct 2026](https://fmino.gov.ng/cooperative-digitalisation-fg-reaffirms-commitment-towards-national-cooperative-identity-reform-under-rh-crrp-2030-tasks-states-on-implementation/); [The Cooperator, Sep 2026](https://thecooperator.news/nigeria-moves-to-digitalise-cooperative-sector-as-government-launches-national-identity-system/)). As of October 2026 no bill text for a new federal Act has reached the National Assembly, so the 1993/2004 Act remains the operative law, and nothing in CBN's microfinance framework forces a large cooperative to convert to a microfinance bank — the 2005/2011 policy says a financial cooperative "can either incorporate a subsidiary MFB ... or transform to a MFB", which is permissive ([CBN Microfinance Policy 2011](https://www.cbn.gov.ng/Out/2011/publications/dfd/Reviewed%20Microfinance%20Policy%20July%2012%202011.pdf)). Chevron's CEMCS has in fact done so voluntarily, running a licensed Chevron Employee Co-operative MFB beside the society ([BusinessList](https://www.businesslist.com.ng/company/100557/chevron-employee-co-operative-mfb)).

## A signed deduction authority and a tripartite agreement hold the model together

The payroll check-off is the economic engine of a staff cooperative and it has no dedicated statutory basis. The Labour Act prohibits any wage deduction except as permitted, allows an employer "with the consent of a worker" to deduct and "pay to the appropriate person any contributions to provident or pension funds or other schemes agreed to by the worker", and caps total deductions at **one-third of a worker's wages in any month** (s.5(1), (2), (7)) ([Labour Act s.5](https://jurist.ng/labour_act/sec-5)). Nigerian commentary therefore lists "cooperatives dues" among deductions that are illegal unless authorised ([TheNigeriaLawyer](https://thenigerialawyer.com/fines-and-deductions-from-a-workers-salary-are-illegal/)), and payroll-compliance guidance for 2026 requires cooperative contributions to be separately itemised on the payslip ([AnooreHR](https://anoorehr.com/blog/nigeria-payslip-legal-requirements-2026)). Bye-laws make the authority a condition of membership: at NFIU "every member shall sign a letter of authority for the deduction of his/her contribution from his/her monthly salary/pension" and "repayment of every loan defaulter shall be deducted from the salary of the member" ([NFIUCOOP](https://www.nfiu.gov.ng/NFIUCooperative/Files/NFIUCOOP%20BYE%20LAW.pdf)); the ASUU (LASU-Ojo) society's supervisory checklist asks whether each borrower has signed "a special agreement ... authorizing the society to claim from his retiring benefits or gratuities any unpaid balance of the loan in the event of death, retirement or termination" ([ASUU Ojo Bye-Laws](https://asuuojocoop.com/downloads/bye-laws.pdf)). The Act supplies the back-stop: s.16 gives the society a first charge and right of set-off over a present, past or deceased member's shares, deposits, dividends and bonuses, s.17 shields those balances from the member's other creditors, and s.49 deems a debt claim against a member a "dispute touching the business of the society" referable to the Director ([Act ss.16–17, 49](https://faolex.fao.org/docs/pdf/nig120579.pdf)).

The employer is bound only by contract, and that contract is where the model most often breaks. The National Industrial Court's January 2025 judgment in *Oredo LGC Staff Multi-Purpose Cooperative v Oredo Local Government Council* is the clearest statement of the law: under a "tripartite arrangement" the council deducted thrift contributions but stopped remitting after September 2018, accumulating **₦127.46 million by February 2022**; the court held it had jurisdiction because the dispute arose from the workplace and wage conditions, found a breach of the tripartite agreement and of a fiduciary duty owed to staff, and awarded ₦112.46 million (after a ₦15 million part-payment) with 10% pre-judgment interest from 28 February 2022, 10% post-judgment interest and ₦250,000 costs ([NICN, Oredo LGC Staff Coop v Oredo LGC](https://nicnadr.gov.ng/nicnweb/details.php?+3+ORS+-VS-+Oredo+Local+Government+Council=&id=9622&p=Oredo+Local+Government+Council+Staff+)). The same failure recurs at scale: Oyo State Water Corporation staff locked the state Secretariat in February 2021 over cooperative, car-loan and housing-loan deductions that grew from ₦122 million to "over ₦200 million" unremitted ([Tribune](https://tribuneonlineng.com/oyo-workers-protest-govt-failure-to-remit-n200-million-cooperative-deductions/)); Ogun State's salary-earners' cooperative union went 17 to 21 months without remittance in 2019–2022, triggering an indefinite strike ([ThisDay via PressReader](https://www.pressreader.com/nigeria/thisday/20220628/282183654738790)); Kwara Water Corporation withheld about ₦70 million over ten months in 2015 ([Daily Trust via PressReader](https://www.pressreader.com/nigeria/daily-trust/20151028/281651073967645)); and the federal IPPIS platform deducted ₦4.4 million from NBS Kwara staff cooperative members in December 2021 and had not remitted it by November 2024, with the society able to reach IPPIS only by physical letter while some affected members retired or died ([FIJ](https://fij.ng/article/ippis-withholds-nbs-kwara-staff-cooperatives-n4m-since-2022/)). Even the NNPC/PPMC Port Harcourt branch lists "continual delayed remittance of members' contributions deducted from source" among its operating challenges ([PPMC PH Coop AGM Report](https://www.ppmcphcoop.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/AGM-Report-compressed.pdf)).

The analytical point is that a cooperative whose deductions flow through a state treasury or IPPIS is an unsecured creditor of that payer, and Nigeria has no regulator tallying the exposure. Kenya does: SASRA reported **KSh3.49 billion of employer-withheld SACCO deductions in 2024** (85 SACCOs, 55,600 members, 74.5% of it loan repayments, counties holding 46%), rising to KSh3.92 billion and 104,000 members in 2025, and the Treasury is now planning to route SACCO deductions directly from payroll, bypassing employers ([Serrari Group](https://serrarigroup.com/saccos-confront-ksh-3-5-billion-in-unremitted-deductions-raising-liquidity-and-trust-concerns/); [Capital FM](https://capitalfm.africa/counties-public-universities-lead-in-sh3-92bn-unremitted-sacco-deductions/); [Sacco Review](https://saccoreview.co.ke/sacco-deductions-to-skip-employers-in-treasury-payroll-plan/)). The one-third cap is also not operationally enforced across lenders: IPPIS and state payrolls process cooperative, microfinance-bank and fintech deductions independently, so Kano found **21,000 of roughly 61,000 public servants** indebted to private loan firms, many "with minimal or no take-home pay after loan deductions", and suspended all loan deductions for three months in April 2025 ([BusinessDay](https://businessday.ng/news/article/21000-kano-civil-servants-indebted-to-private-loan-firms/)). The safest architecture for a society is therefore a written, revocable member authority; a deduction-and-remittance agreement with the employer specifying the monthly schedule file, a remittance date within days of payday, reconciliation and the duty to remit even when the employer is itself in arrears to staff; payslip itemisation; and a monthly headroom test that keeps each member's aggregate deductions below one-third of gross.

## Bye-laws converge on a 2x-savings, two-guarantor, 25%-reserve template

The Act prescribes what bye-laws must cover — name, objects, membership terms, custody and investment of funds, liability, withdrawal and expulsion, share transfer, general meetings, committee appointment and removal, signing authority and disposal of surplus, plus for lending societies the interest rate, maximum loan, maximum tenor, permitted purposes and consequences of default (s.11(2)–(3)) — and makes amendments invalid until registered by the Director (s.12) ([Act ss.11–12](https://faolex.fao.org/docs/pdf/nig120579.pdf)). The two fully read staff-cooperative bye-laws, one FCT-registered (NFIU) and one Lagos-registered (ASUU-Ojo), converge so closely with the CBN, Accion MFB, Niger Delta University and UNN rules that they can be treated as a de facto national template.

Three features distinguish staff societies from the generic cooperative governance described in the earlier report. The employer's "patron" role is not a statutory concept but is built in through an Advisory Committee of senior management — at NFIU chaired by an officer not below GL15 with two Heads of Department, sitting above the elected committee in the dispute ladder (MC → Advisory Committee → Chief Registrar arbitration) ([NFIUCOOP](https://www.nfiu.gov.ng/NFIUCooperative/Files/NFIUCOOP%20BYE%20LAW.pdf)) — while the societies themselves insist on legal independence: the CBN society states it "is not a subsidiary of the Central Bank of Nigeria" ([CBN Coop](https://www.thecbncoop.com/about)). The registrar is embedded in the audit: NFIU appoints one external auditor itself and receives a second from the FCT Cooperative Secretariat whose statutory report "shall be the final audit exercise", pays 1% of surplus as an audit-and-supervision fee, and runs a fixed compliance calendar (accounts to 31 December, published by 31 March, three copies to the Registrar by 31 July before the AGM, work plan by 31 October, budget by 30 November). And related-party and custody controls are explicit: ASUU-Ojo requires general-meeting approval for related-party transactions above ₦100,000 or 10% of assets, fidelity bonding of cash-handling officers and a bank mandate of any two of three trustees ([ASUU Ojo](https://asuuojocoop.com/downloads/bye-laws.pdf)).

The supervisor's powers are administrative and wide. The Director may audit, access all books and cash at any reasonable time and prescribe returns (s.36); must hold an inquiry on application by a majority of the committee or one-third of members, and "if an inquiry ... proves that the committee ... is inefficient" may **take over management for two years** (s.37); may cancel registration if membership falls below ten or after inquiry (s.38), and no society may be wound up except by the Director's or a court's order; may surcharge any officer who misapplied funds or committed breach of trust and order interim attachment of property (ss.46–48); and must receive every dispute "touching the business of a registered society" for settlement or arbitration, with awards enforceable as civil judgments and appeal to the Commissioner whose decision is "final and conclusive" (ss.49–50) ([Act](https://faolex.fao.org/docs/pdf/nig120579.pdf)). Misapplication of society property and breach of the surplus rules are offences, though the fine floors (₦1,000 or six months) are 1993 figures. Lagos adds a statutory Cooperative Dispute Resolution Panel under s.60 of its 2022 Law and Inspectorate, Mediation, Insurance and Monitoring/Enforcement units across 24 area offices for its 16,000+ registered societies ([Lagos MCCTI](https://mccti.lagosstate.gov.ng/departments/cooperatives)). Affiliation runs upward from state union or financing agency to state federation (LASCOFED in Lagos, ABCOF in the FCT) to the National Cooperative Financing Agency (CFAN, the 2015 rebrand of NACCUN, founded 1978) and the Cooperative Federation of Nigeria, with CFAN affiliated to ACCOSCA and NACCUN listed as a WOCCU member; CFAN offers wholesale liquidity, training, insurance and mortgage linkages, and bye-laws require affiliation "unless exempted by the Registrar" ([CFAN](https://www.cfan.coop/about-cfan/); [WOCCU membership](https://www.woccu.org/member_services/our_network/membership)). What the system lacks is any deposit protection — cooperative savings are not NDIC-insured ([KudiCompass](https://kudicompass.com/how-to-join-cooperative-society-nigeria/)) — and any prudential ratio regime of the SASRA type, which is why no Nigerian society publishes an NPL, capital or liquidity ratio.

## Membership follows the payslip: join on confirmation, borrow in year two, exit by set-off

Eligibility tracks employment status more finely than the Act requires. The Act's only tests are age 16+ and residence or occupation of land within the area of operation, with the land test waived for credit societies (s.22) ([Act s.22](https://faolex.fao.org/docs/pdf/nig120579.pdf)); bye-laws then layer on confirmation and contract status. The CBN society admits serving staff, pensioners and retirees but grants credit only to "confirmed staff with 12+ months continuous contributions" — "unconfirmed staff are ineligible" ([CBN Coop](https://www.thecbncoop.com/service/view/1)). Accion MFB's society creates an Associate class for contract staff who "may not be granted credit facilities ... in excess of the member's net savings" with repayment "not more than the contract end date", and terminates membership on "exit from AMfB payroll" ([Accion MFB](http://accionmfbcoop.com.ng/assets/files/bye_law.pdf)). Seconded staff at NFIU need two confirmed-staff guarantors with at least five years to serve plus a parent-organisation undertaking; former staff may borrow "below 70% of his contribution or savings" with guarantors ten years from retirement, and retired members "shall not vie for or hold any position in the MC" ([NFIUCOOP](https://www.nfiu.gov.ng/NFIUCooperative/Files/NFIUCOOP%20BYE%20LAW.pdf)). Niger Delta University lets a leaver "retain his membership ... but may not borrow ... in excess of his Assets" and requires committee aspirants to be confirmed university staff ([NDU](https://nducoop.com/uploads/Bye-Laws.pdf)). The pattern is consistent: savings are open to almost anyone on or formerly on the payroll; leverage is reserved for those the payroll can still reach.

The lifecycle inside a society has a predictable shape. A new member pays a ₦2,000–₦5,000 entrance fee, subscribes minimum shares of about ₦50,000 by instalment and begins compulsory savings of ₦5,000–₦20,000 a month; after six to twelve months the first loan becomes available at two to three times savings, so the first meaningful loan usually arrives in year two; while any loan is outstanding the savings are locked as collateral and, at ASUU-Ojo, the committee may suspend ordinary savings "during the currency of a loan" where repayment "may impose more hardship" ([ASUU Ojo](https://asuuojocoop.com/downloads/bye-laws.pdf)). Loan stacking is addressed by the Act's bar on belonging to more than one loan-granting society without consent (s.24) and by ASUU-Ojo's requirement that each applicant render "a true and complete statement of his indebtedness outside the society", but there is no cross-lender bureau — the planned CoopCheck credit bureau in the federal digital stack is the first attempt ([The Cooperator](https://thecooperator.news/nigeria-moves-to-digitalise-cooperative-sector-as-government-launches-national-identity-system/)).

Exit is slow by design. Withdrawal requires one to three months' notice and is barred while the member "is in any form indebted to the Society" or stands surety; NFIU caps voluntary savings withdrawals at 60% once a year and only after all loans are cleared, limits share withdrawals to 5% of applications a year without the Chief Registrar's permission, and charges a ₦20,000 administrative fee; UNN charges a 10% service charge on withdrawal of ordinary savings; past members remain liable for society debts incurred before cessation (NFIU six months; Act s.29 two years) ([NFIUCOOP](https://www.nfiu.gov.ng/NFIUCooperative/Files/NFIUCOOP%20BYE%20LAW.pdf); [UNN FAQs](https://www.unn.edu.ng/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/FAQs.pdf); [Act s.29](https://faolex.fao.org/docs/pdf/nig120579.pdf)). On dismissal or resignation, "entitlements in the Society, if any, or his/her guarantor shall be called upon to repay the outstanding loan"; on death, s.18 transfers the interest to the nominee, who receives "the value of the share or interest, less any sum due to the society" ([NFIUCOOP](https://www.nfiu.gov.ng/NFIUCooperative/Files/NFIUCOOP%20BYE%20LAW.pdf); [Accion MFB](http://accionmfbcoop.com.ng/assets/files/bye_law.pdf)). The closest NICN authority on post-exit recovery, *Asana v First Bank*, concerned an employer staff loan rather than a cooperative loan, but held that "the obligation to repay the staff loan survives the termination" while refusing to let a concessional rate be re-priced to commercial after constructive dismissal ([Mondaq](https://www.mondaq.com/nigeria/employment-litigation-tribunals/760262/is-a-terminateddismissed-worker-obliged-to-repay-staff-loans)). Two consequences follow. A member's savings are effectively illiquid collateral, and a well-run society's exit refunds still take months; a badly run one converts exit into a multi-year dispute. And retirees are the most exposed class: they lose the payroll link, their balances are subject to set-off, and where investments have been built from members' money, leavers receive only savings and share balances with no share of the estate or portfolio ([RUN Repository](https://repository.run.edu.ng/bitstreams/3761bb58-bfef-410e-a2d5-2ec08850af24/download)) — a point that should shape how a society accounts for revaluation gains on member-funded property.

Participation data are thin but suggest near-universal uptake where a society exists. At FUTA Akure in 2009, **272 of 297 surveyed staff (92%)** belonged to one of eight campus cooperatives ([Adedeji & Olotuah, ESJ 2012](https://eujournal.org/index.php/esj/article/download/181/185)). Institution sizes range from 355 members at the PPMC Port Harcourt branch and 1,315 at NLNG through 2,000 at Unique UNILAG and ExxonMobil, 2,924 at Ambrose Alli University, 3,000 at Shell COOPLAG, 4,455 at Immigration, 7,606 at NNPC Lagos and over 9,000 at the CBN ([NLNG Coop](https://nlng.coop.ng/About); [COOPLAG](https://cooplag.com.ng/About); [NNPC Coop](https://nnpccoop.coop/); [CBN Coop](https://www.thecbncoop.com/about)). The dominant motive in Nigerian cooperative surveys is collateral-free credit — 62–63% of EFInA's 700 members, with low interest (37%), social-event support (36%) and lump-sum saving (32%) next ([EFInA 2012 Key Findings](https://www.efina.org.ng/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/Understanding-Co-operatives-in-NigeriaKeyFindings.pdf)); for university staff the housing-loan and emergency-finance motives dominate ([Okafor et al., IJMRGE 2025](https://www.allmultidisciplinaryjournal.com/uploads/archives/20250101175012_F-24-252.1.pdf)). Non-joining is undocumented in Nigeria; Kenya's FinAccess finds affordability the top barrier for 54.5% of non-users and eligibility the main barrier for the young and unemployed ([FSD Kenya SACCO Report, Nov 2025](https://www.fsdkenya.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/SACCO-Subsector-Report-Lessons-from-FinAccess-Surveys.pdf)), which maps onto Nigerian minimums of ₦10,000–₦20,000 a month being a large share of junior-grade pay.

## Thrift and credit earns the spread; housing earns the capital gain

### Loan pricing splits into subsidised and bank-like regimes

Every staff society runs the same credit engine — compulsory savings, a 2x–3x multiple, two guarantors, payroll recovery and a payroll headroom test — but pricing varies by a factor of four. The CBN society's 2020 schedule charges **5.0% on a 12-month normal loan** (2× net savings), 12.5% on a special loan (5× savings), 3–6% on one- to six-month short-term loans, 5% on commodity loans, 11.75% on 48-month investment loans and 7.5% on three-month LPO finance, disburses in 24–48 hours and sets capacity "by grade level and exposure" ([CBN Coop](https://www.thecbncoop.com/service/view/1); [CBN Coop FAQ](https://www.thecbncoop.com/faq)). Shell's CoopEast prices like a bank: regular loans to ₦15 million at 21% flat over 36 months with 20% savings backing and "45% headroom in payroll", mortgages to ₦40 million at 21% reducing over five years, car loans to ₦20 million at 19–21% over four years, short-term loans at 2.5% flat per month, and a single-obligor cap at the member's gross annual basic ([CoopEast Loan Schemes](https://www.coopeastngr.com/loanscheme.asp)). Universities sit between: UNN lends three times savings at 11% (one year) to 19% (emergency), collects interest and a 1% handling charge up-front, and marks up commodities 7% ([UNN FAQs](https://www.unn.edu.ng/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/FAQs.pdf)); Unique UNILAG charges 10% over up to 24 months; FRCN Lagos 8.5–12% on normal loans and 20% on a ₦2 million home-ownership/vehicle scheme ([FRCN Lagos MCS](https://www.frcnlagoscms.org/web/home/update2.php?idupdate=2)). Against a 26.5–27.25% monetary policy rate and bank consumer credit that fell 19.9% to ₦3.78 trillion in 2025 ([Nairametrics, July 2026](http://nairametrics.com/2026/07/29/nigerias-consumer-credit-falls-to-n3-8-trillion-first-decline-in-six-years/)), even the bank-like regime is cheap, and the subsidised regime is a transfer from savers to borrowers funded by cross-subsidy from investment income.

Default is treated in the literature as a governance rather than an underwriting problem because the guarantor-plus-payroll structure makes credit loss rare where the payroll holds: Total E&P's society reported a five-year NPL of **0.157%** ([stories.coop](https://stories.coop/a-growing-coop-in-nigeria/)). Where the payroll does not hold, default rises fast — Lagos research finds employment instability and irregular income significantly raise default and that defaults "eroded capital reserves and member equity" ([MAJAF](https://majaf.com.ng/index.php/majaf/article/view/306)); an Ekiti study calls default "a dominant issue that has discouraged most cooperative societies from granting loan", with loan diversion exerting a negative effect on growth ([EKSU BF Journal](https://bfjournal.eksu.edu.ng/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/loan-default.pdf)); and EFInA's managers reported that "members often do not pay back their loans on time and this depletes the cash", with 42% of borrowers carrying balances ([EFInA 2012](https://www.efina.org.ng/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/Understanding-Co-operatives-in-NigeriaKeyFindings.pdf)). No Nigerian society publishes an NPL ratio; Kenya's regulated sector averaged 8.2% in 2024 ([Sacco Review](https://saccoreview.co.ke/unpacking-sasra-supervision-report-2024-a-sector-in-resilient-expansion/)).

### The surplus waterfall leaves roughly half for members, and real returns are often negative

The accountant's view of a staff cooperative is a waterfall fixed by statute and bye-law. Act s.34 requires "at least one fourth of the net profits" into an indivisible reserve fund (the Director may lower it for limited-liability societies), permits up to 10% of the remainder to an education fund with the Director's approval, bars any dividend or bonus until the committee approves the appropriation, and criminalises wilful breach ([Act s.34](https://faolex.fao.org/docs/pdf/nig120579.pdf)). The NFIU schedule shows a complete allocation of 100 units of net surplus: **25 reserve, 7 education and training, 1 audit and supervision fee to the Chief Registrar, 7.5 honoraria, 2 retained earnings, 4.5 meeting expenses, 1 community development, 52 dividends** ([NFIUCOOP](https://www.nfiu.gov.ng/NFIUCooperative/Files/NFIUCOOP%20BYE%20LAW.pdf)); ASUU-Ojo's version is 25% reserve, 2.5% education, honoraria capped at 7.5% and a dividend floor of 50%, all "subject to approval by the Director" ([ASUU Ojo](https://asuuojocoop.com/downloads/bye-laws.pdf)). The dividend itself is split three ways in practice — interest on savings, dividend on share capital and a patronage bonus "in proportion to the volume of business done", the UNN distinction between bonus and dividend ([UNN FAQs](https://www.unn.edu.ng/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/FAQs.pdf)) — and the only general statutory dividend cap (5% p.a.) applies in the liquidation waterfall of s.44, not to a going concern.

Published AGM figures confirm the payout ratios and, more importantly, the yields. INEC's staff society earned ₦52.5 million, spent ₦18.0 million, declared a **₦34.5 million surplus for 2024** and provided ₦22.0 million for dividend, a 64% payout ([Gazette Nigeria](https://gazettengr.com/inec-staff-cooperative-records-n34-million-surplus-for-2024/)). The Nigerian Shippers' Council society reported a ₦181.22 million surplus for 2025, paid ₦90.61 million in dividends (50%) directly to bank accounts and ₦102.4 million to retirees "without deductions" ([Tribune](https://tribuneonlineng.com/nsc-cooperative-declares-n181-2m-surplus-unveils-new-leadership/)). The News Agency of Nigeria society is the one case that discloses rates: on a ₦34.07 million surplus it distributed ₦23.5 million (69%) as **₦0.19 per share and ₦0.03 per ₦1 of savings** against members' savings of ₦438.69 million and share capital of ₦50.78 million ([NAN](https://nannews.ng/nan-staff-cooperative-declares-n34m-surplus-for-2025/)). The PPMC Port Harcourt branch paid 2% interest on savings, a 10% dividend on share capital and a 0.5% patronage bonus on a ₦597 million savings base ([PPMC PH AGM Report](https://www.ppmcphcoop.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/AGM-Report-compressed.pdf)). A 3% return on savings in a year when headline inflation ran at 33–35% ([Proshare](https://www.proshare.co/articles/nigeria-march-2024-inflation-rises-to-33.20-the-options-for-households?menu=Economy&classification=Read&category=Nigeria+Economy)) is a real loss of roughly 30 percentage points, and a Redeemer's University survey found 58.8% of members considered the dividend "not enough" while 79.2% opposed raising loan rates to fund it ([RUN Repository](https://repository.run.edu.ng/bitstreams/3761bb58-bfef-410e-a2d5-2ec08850af24/download)). The member's true return is the option to borrow cheaply, not the yield on savings; a society that prices loans at 5% is paying its borrowers with its savers' purchasing power, which is defensible only if most members borrow most years. Kenya's comparison is stark: Mwalimu paid 10% on deposits and 13% on shares and Stima 16% on shares plus an 11% interest rebate for 2024 against roughly 5% inflation ([Mwango Capital](https://x.com/MwangoCapital/status/1904794285024924038); [The Star](https://www.the-star.co.ke/business/kenya/2025-02-28-stima-sacco-declares-16-per-cent-dividend)).

### Housing turns a thrift society into a land bank, with two acquisition models

Housing is the aspirational product and the only line where member wealth creation has been extraordinary. The oil-sector model is bulk acquisition of titled land, early. Chevron's CEMCS bought the majority of Nicon Town's plots from Nicon Lekki Ltd in 2001, completed the 57-hectare, 431-plot estate in 2007 and allocated plots to members at about **₦5 million; the plots are now valued at ₦160–200 million** ([Estate Intel](https://estateintel.com/insights/real-estate-developments-owned-by-cooperative-societies-in-nigeria); [OwnAHome.ng](https://ownahome.ng/blog/what-is-so-special-about-nicon-town-estate-lekki)); it followed with the 14-hectare Melrose Park. Shell's COOPLAG built COOPLAG Gardens on Orchid Road (resale ₦125–190 million per duplex) and The Autograph on Banana Island Road (84 units, financed by Cooperative Mortgage Bank) ([Nigeria Property Centre](https://nigeriapropertycentre.com/for-sale/houses/lagos/lekki/showtype?n=cooplag+gardens+estate); [CMBank](https://cmbankng.com/financed-projects/ongoing-projects/autograph-banana-island/)). The joint-venture variant is CoopEast Beach Resort: 101 units on three hectares costing ₦5.6 billion, owned 50% by Shell's CoopEast, 20% by ExxonMobil's EMCOOP and 30% by individuals, built by Brains and Hammers Plus between August 2019 and June 2022 ([Vanguard](https://www.vanguardngr.com/2022/06/developer-delivers-n5-6bn-residential-estate-for-exxonmobil-shell-cooperatives/)); TotalEnergies' housing cooperative, formed 2020, is building a 157-apartment tower in Eko Atlantic with a US$40 million FCMB facility ([Premium Times](https://www.premiumtimesng.com/promoted/807980-fcmb-backs-totalenergies-staff-housing-cooperative-multipurpose-society-ltd-tehc-flagship-project-with-40m.html)).

Public-sector societies cannot self-fund and use the Federal Mortgage Bank's cooperative windows: the Cooperative Housing Development Loan lends up to **₦500 million at 10% p.a.** over 24 months with a 12-month moratorium to societies with unencumbered title, and members take NHF mortgages of up to ₦15 million at 9% over up to 30 years; Akacare's Woodhill Estate in Kuje (230 units, ₦997 million of FMBN funding, ₦7.5–9.5 million per unit) had a member paying ₦3.5 million equity with the balance "deducted from salary until retirement" ([ThisDay, 2021](https://www.thisdaylive.com/2021/08/11/how-fmbn-is-leveraging-co-operatives-to-make-homes-possible-for-nigerians/)). The Police cooperative has ₦4.74 billion of FMBN development loans approved for 606 units ([Economic Times NG](https://theeconomictimes.com.ng/2026/01/29/fmbn-disburses-over-%E2%82%A67bn-in-mortgages-cooperative-housing-for-police-officers-osidi/)); Immigration's NIMCOS commissioned 150 bungalows at Gwagwalada in July 2026 as part of a 1,500-unit scheme ([BusinessDay](https://businessday.ng/uncategorized/article/fg-inaugurates-1500-unit-housing-estate-for-immigration-personnel-in-abuja/)); NMDPRA's society placed 430 staff in Renewed Hope estates with ₦8.3 billion of NHF mortgages packaged through a mortgage bank, against more than 1,000 applicants ([BusinessDay](https://businessday.ng/companies/article/nmdpra-workers-tap-n8-3bn-fmbn-mortgage-pipeline-for-homes/)); Customs partnered Cooperative Mortgage Bank for 5,009 units ([Vanguard](https://www.vanguardngr.com/2023/12/customs-mortgage-bank-partner-on-5009-housing-units-for-officers/)). Delivery for ordinary members is partial: Lagos institution-based cooperatives delivered on subscriptions for 38.2% (land), 23.8% (housing development) and 43.3% (bulk materials) of members, and a 2026 study found only 27.2% of housing-cooperative members achieved their housing objective ([ResearchGate, Lagos housing](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/319321364_Empirical_Assessment_of_Success_Rate_of_Co-operative_Societies'_Housing_Provision_in_Lagos_State_Nigeria); [IJRISS 2026](https://rsisinternational.org/journals/ijriss/uploads/vol10-iss6-pg3334-3360-202606_pdf.pdf)). Against a housing deficit above 20 million units, cooperative output in the low thousands is negligible nationally but decisive for the members who get a plot.

### Consumer, investment, welfare and enterprise lines are extensions of the same balance sheet

Consumer lines are credit with goods substituted for cash: a "commodity loan" at the normal rate (CBN 5%; CoopEast ₦2 million at 21% reducing over 12 months) or a cost-plus mark-up (UNN 7%), recovered by payroll, with no inventory risk for most societies; only the largest run owned outlets — Total E&P's Coop Mart, Café, Laundry, Travels, Guesthouse and Lube Bay, NLNG's online stores ([stories.coop](https://stories.coop/a-growing-coop-in-nigeria/); [NLNG Coop](https://nlng.coop.ng/About)). Investment is governed by Act s.33, which permits deposits in a cooperative or approved bank, Federal Government securities "and ... any other manner approved by the Committee", an open clause bounded only by bye-laws and the Director; actual portfolios run from fixed deposits and landed property (UNILAG, which raised ₦1 billion in a 2019 share issue of 500 million units at ₦2) to the CBN society's **14% four-year member investment notes of ₦5–50 million** that fund the loan book below lending rates ([UNILAG news](https://unilag.edu.ng/?p=4868); [CBN Coop FAQ](https://www.thecbncoop.com/faq)). Members are ambivalent: 58.3% prefer land and buildings to shares or deposits, yet 75.8% oppose outside investments altogether ([RUN Repository](https://repository.run.edu.ng/bitstreams/3761bb58-bfef-410e-a2d5-2ec08850af24/download)). Welfare is financed from members' own ring-fenced pots (UNN education savings at 5%, retirement savings at 4%, Christmas savings at 4%), the education fund and guarantor-funded write-offs on death, not from insurance; an Ibadan survey found only 30.8% of cooperatives had any health budget ([UNN FAQs](https://www.unn.edu.ng/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/FAQs.pdf); [IAR Consortium](https://iarconsortium.org/srjm/47/202/35_Cooperative_Societies_as_Sources_of_Health_Insurance_among_members_in_Ibadan_Nigeria/)). Enterprise ventures with disclosed figures exist only where there is hired professional management and a captive high-income market — Total E&P's society reported PAT above ₦1 billion for two to three years and **₦2.25 billion in 2017 on about 1,500 members** ([stories.coop](https://stories.coop/a-growing-coop-in-nigeria/)); elsewhere bakeries, transport fleets and schools remain bye-law aspirations.

## Members gain cheap credit and lose liquidity; employers gain loyalty and inherit remittance liability

For staff the advantages are concrete and the costs structural. The member obtains collateral-free credit at 5–21% when the policy rate is above 26% and banks are retrenching consumer lending, a commitment-savings device enforced by payroll, a path to land or housing that no mortgage market offers a GL8 officer, a cash dividend most years, and death and emergency cover through guarantors and the education fund. The costs are illiquidity (savings locked while borrowing, 60% annual withdrawal caps, administrative charges on exit), a negative real return on the savings component, exclusion of unconfirmed and contract staff from leverage, no deposit insurance, exposure to committee fraud and regulator paralysis, and a systemic over-indebtedness risk once cooperative deductions sit beside microfinance and fintech deductions on the same payslip — a GL14 officer with 24 years' service told Daily Trust "I earn, but I hardly see my money. Loans finish everything", and the Federal Government Staff Housing Loans Board says "heavy loan deductions render workers ineligible for housing loans" ([Daily Trust](https://dailytrust.com/high-cost-of-living-pushes-abuja-civil-servants-into-debt/)). A real and underappreciated risk is the asymmetry at retirement: NNPC retirees report no dividends for roughly ten years and no access to savings for six ([Vanguard](https://www.vanguardngr.com/2025/05/nnpc-retirees-cry-out-over-possible-loss-of-savings-with-staff-cooperative/)), and a retired CSP with 35 years' service was told the Police cooperative "could only pay contributions from 2019, the year they computerised their system" because earlier records were "mutilated" ([FIJ](https://fij.ng/article/police-cooperative-society-withholds-retired-csps-savings-because-it-mishandled-his-records/)).

For the employer the case is weaker than HR folklore suggests. The documented benefits are a near-zero-cost welfare benefit — "handled well, a salary advance or staff loan is a low-cost benefit that builds loyalty" ([AnooreHR](https://anoorehr.com/blog/salary-advances-staff-loans-payroll-nigeria)), soft loans being "highly valued benefits in the current high-interest market" ([Ribi People](https://ribipeople.com/resources/employee-benefits-in-nigeria-the-complete-2026-guide)) — plus the diversion of emergency-credit demand away from the employer's own salary-advance book and, for parastatals, a vehicle for staff housing that the Interior Minister or Inspector-General can commission as a welfare achievement. No Nigerian study measures retention, absenteeism or productivity effects attributable to a staff cooperative; the only indirect evidence is Kenyan, where SACCO members' health is "generally better than that of the average Kenyan", attributed to employment status rather than membership ([FSD Kenya](https://www.fsdkenya.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/SACCO-Subsector-Report-Lessons-from-FinAccess-Surveys.pdf)). The employer's costs are definite: payroll administration of a monthly deduction file and reconciliation, fiduciary liability for deducted-but-unremitted sums with 10% interest (*Oredo*), industrial action when remittance fails (Oyo, Ogun), and reputational contamination when an in-house society collapses — NNPC members publicly demand that "NNPC Ltd management" intervene in a society that is legally independent of it ([Vanguard](https://www.vanguardngr.com/2025/05/nnpc-retirees-cry-out-over-possible-loss-of-savings-with-staff-cooperative/)). Restructuring is a further exposure: PPMC's Port Harcourt branch lost 8% of members to redeployment in a year, and Shell's 2025 sale of SPDC left cooperatives whose eligibility is tied to Shell employment to redefine their common bond ([PPMC PH AGM Report](https://www.ppmcphcoop.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/AGM-Report-compressed.pdf); [Shell](https://www.shell.com/news-and-insights/newsroom/news-and-media-releases/2025/shell-completes-sale-of-spdc.html)).

## Measured effects are unanimous in direction and weak in design

Every Nigerian study of staff-cooperative members reports positive effects, and none has a counterfactual. A 2025 study of five universities' staff in Delta State (population 110, sample 86) found members "have higher savings and access to credit" and that membership helps "access housings loans, which improve the living standard", with a grand mean of 4.2 on savings items ([Okafor et al., IJMRGE 2025](https://www.allmultidisciplinaryjournal.com/uploads/archives/20250101175012_F-24-252.1.pdf)); Ajayi and Chilokwu's regression on 355 Ekiti university staff found "access to educational loans, land acquisition, and household asset purchases significantly improved staff welfare"; Olujimi's 683 Akure house-owners showed salary deductions funded housing loans and lending criteria did not significantly hinder access ([IJSBER 2025](https://eajournals.org/ijsber/wp-content/uploads/sites/85/2025/01/Effect-of-Cooperative-Thrift.pdf)). In Awka South, simple regressions on ten thrift societies gave credit disbursement a coefficient of 11.66 and savings mobilisation 1.61 on member income, both significant, while financial education was not ([IJSBER 2025](https://eajournals.org/ijsber/wp-content/uploads/sites/85/2025/01/Effect-of-Cooperative-Thrift.pdf)). The only effect sizes with non-member comparisons come from rural and trader cooperatives — members' incomes about 10% higher in the South-West and 28% higher among Anambra farmers ([ResearchGate, South-West](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/351705321_Impact_of_Cooperative_Membership_on_Rural_Income_Generation_in_Southwest_Nigeria); [ResearchGate, Anambra](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/282325374_Effects_Of_Membership_Of_Cooperative_Organisations_And_Determinants_On_Farmer-_Members'_Income_In_Rural_Anambra_State_Nigeria)) — and EFInA's 2012 member survey shows the typical use of a cooperative loan (business stock 61%, emergencies 24%, land or house 8%) with an average loan of ₦33,236 and disbursement in one to two weeks ([EFInA 2012](https://www.efina.org.ng/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/Understanding-Co-operatives-in-NigeriaKeyFindings.pdf)).

The honest reading is that the "higher savings" finding is partly tautological (savings are compulsory for members), selection is unaddressed (better-off staff join and stay), and the outcomes are self-reported. Housing, the most-cited benefit, reaches a minority — 20% of FUTA staff lived in their own homes against 58–67% renting even with eight campus cooperatives, and the authors concluded that "the level of accessibility of low-income earners to housing finance in Nigeria is still very low despite the intermediation of ... cooperative societies" ([Adedeji & Olotuah](https://eujournal.org/index.php/esj/article/download/181/185)). Kenya's larger surveys sharpen the picture without changing it: SACCO membership is highest among the formally employed and tertiary-educated (26.0% usage versus 7.8% for primary-educated), loans go to education (34.2%) and land/housing (26.7%), urban men get larger loans, the number of members borrowing has fallen since 2019 while average loan size rose, and **75.1% of members who lost money blamed internal fraud** ([FSD Kenya](https://www.fsdkenya.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/SACCO-Subsector-Report-Lessons-from-FinAccess-Surveys.pdf)). The research gap that matters most for a Nigerian employer is the absence of any study using payroll administrative data to compare members and non-members on retention, salary-advance requests or indebtedness; the data exist in every HR system and have never been analysed.

## ₦1.7 trillion is claimed, nothing is audited, and Nigeria is one-eighth of Kenya

Nigeria's cooperative sector has no measured size. The supervising minister's figures — "over 370,000 registered cooperative societies, more than 30 million members" and cooperative assets of "₦1.7 trillion" to be grown to ₦5 trillion in five years ([FMINO, Oct 2026](https://fmino.gov.ng/cooperative-digitalisation-fg-reaffirms-commitment-towards-national-cooperative-identity-reform-under-rh-crrp-2030-tasks-states-on-implementation/)) — are not backed by any published aggregate, the government itself lists "capturing the true value of the cooperative sector in GDP" as a future goal, and the ₦1.7 trillion has been reported both as "assets" and as "annual turnover". The historical federal CODAS database enlisted just 19,786 societies, a private aggregator estimates 30,000 societies and 5–7 million members, CFAN claims 5.2 million credit-cooperative members in 2,040 groups and concedes that determining savings "currently is difficult due to absence of software", and EFInA's 2010 estimate was 80,000 groups and 1.4 million members ([Federal Department of Cooperatives](http://federaldepartmentofcooperatives.blogspot.com/p/structure-organization-and.html); [cooperatives.com](https://www.cooperatives.com/countries/nigeria); [CFAN](https://www.cfan.coop/about-cfan/); [EFInA 2012](https://www.efina.org.ng/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/Understanding-Co-operatives-in-NigeriaKeyFindings.pdf)). Lagos illustrates the gap between register and reality: 16,000+ societies registered, 2,359 revalidated and 807 accounts approved in a year, a capital base reported as ₦33 billion by the Commissioner in May 2021 and "over ₦180 billion" by the Cooperative College a month earlier ([Nairametrics](https://nairametrics.com/2021/05/18/co-operative-service-capital-base-in-lagos-rises-to-n33-billion-commissioner/); [BusinessDay](https://businessday.ng/opinion/article/enhancing-cooperative-movement-in-nigeria-the-lagos-model/)). The most plausible reconciliation is that 370,000 sums cumulative state registers including dormant societies, that the active, filing share is perhaps 15% as in Lagos, and that ₦33 billion is the capital of societies that actually filed. Oyo reports 7,865 functioning societies; Bayelsa 43 ([InsideOyo](https://insideoyo.com/oyo-commissioner-adebisi-highlights-achievements-in-trade-investment-industry/); [The Whistler](https://thewhistler.ng/fg-moves-to-transform-cooperative-sector-with-bank-digital-registry/)).

There is no count of staff cooperatives at all; no regulator distinguishes employer-based from community societies. What is known is that the disclosed balance sheets are concentrated in a handful of payroll-based societies — TotalEnergies' upstream society at **over ₦66.3 billion** with about 1,400 members (up from ₦32.8 billion in 2017), NLNG at ₦23 billion with 1,315, NAN at ₦439 million of savings, PPMC Port Harcourt at ₦597 million with 355 ([Total Upstream Coop](https://www.totalupstreamng.coop/about-us/our-history/); [NLNG Coop](https://nlng.coop.ng/About); [NAN](https://nannews.ng/nan-staff-cooperative-declares-n34m-surplus-for-2025/)) — and that Kenya's data show employer-based ("government-based") SACCOs holding **64.5% of regulated assets with 37% of members** ([Kenyan Wall Street](https://kenyanwallstreet.com/fragile-sacco-funding-2024)). By analogy, payroll-deduction societies probably hold most of Nigeria's cooperative financial assets while being a small minority of registered societies, but this is inference. Context places the claimed ₦1.7 trillion between microfinance banks (₦2.8 trillion of assets at June 2024, ₦1.3 trillion of deposits, over 900 licences, 46 revoked in July 2026) and banks' ₦3.78 trillion of consumer credit ([Agusto 2025](https://www.agustoresearch.com/report/2025-nigerias-microfinance-industry-report/); [Channels TV](https://www.channelstv.com/2026/07/01/cbn-revokes-operating-licences-of-46-microfinance-banks-full-list/)); if 30 million members and ₦1.7 trillion are both taken at face value, average assets per member are about ₦57,000, confirming that most Nigerian cooperatives are small thrift groups and that staff societies are the outliers.

The benchmark conclusion is uncomfortable. Nigeria, with roughly four times Kenya's population, has a cooperative-finance sector at best one-eighth of Kenya's in dollar assets, is entirely absent from WOCCU's Africa country tables for 2022 and 2024 (appearing only as a member organisation), and after the 2023–24 devaluation has seen its dollar value roughly halve ([WOCCU 2024](https://www.woccu.org/documents/2024_Statistical_Report_EN)). Nominal growth is bifurcated: Lagos's reported capital base rose 3% over two years (a steep real decline) while Total's society doubled, which is consistent with payroll societies thriving and community thrift groups being squeezed by 33–35% inflation. Policy is now trying to import Kenya's and Rwanda's institutions at once — a ₦200 billion Cooperative Bank of Nigeria owned 65% by cooperatives, 30% by institutional investors and 5% by employees, with larger societies asked for ₦21–50 million each and share subscription targeted from end-October 2026 ([MSME Africa](https://msmeafricaonline.com/fg-launches-n200bn-capital-mobilization-drive-for-proposed-cooperative-bank/); [AllAfrica](https://allafrica.com/stories/202605180292.html)), a national registry and credit bureau, Lagos's ₦10 billion LASMECO fund lending to cooperatives at 9% ([MSME Africa](https://msmeafricaonline.com/lagos-expands-msme-financing-with-n10bn-cooperative-fund/)), and a crowded private software market (SmartTeller, Coopra, Cooperativ, Coopify, Sterling's CloudCoop partnership with LASCOFED). Rwanda's sequence — automate all 416 SACCOs (completed June 2024), consolidate into district SACCOs, then a BNR-regulated Cooperative Bank ([MINECOFIN](https://www.minecofin.gov.rw/news-detail/automation-of-416-umurenge-saccos-completed)) — is the explicit template, and Mwalimu's Spire Bank loss (KSh10 billion on a KSh2.4 billion bank acquisition, written off over 2022–27) is the explicit warning that a cooperative bank is a strategic-risk instrument even under a real regulator ([Business Daily](https://www.businessdailyafrica.com/bd/data-hub/teachers-learn-sh10bn-loss-lesson-in-spire-deal--4108192)).

Three cases deserve a closer look because they overturn intuitions. The NLNG fraud shows that outsider land fraud, not insider theft, is the costliest single-event risk: the society paid ₦520 million and ₦130 million in October–November 2011 through an intermediary for 643 hectares in Idu-Sabo, Abuja, that the vendor had no authority to sell; the vendor was convicted only in 2025 after a ten-year trial, two co-defendants died, the society's former president and vice-president charged alongside were discharged or died, and the charged sum is reported inconsistently across outlets from ₦207 million to ₦2.7 billion ([TheNigeriaLawyer](https://thenigerialawyer.com/%E2%82%A6580m-restitution-ordered-after-10-year-trial-court-convicts-and-sentences-wallang-for-%E2%82%A6700m-land-scam-targeting-nlng-staff-cooperative/); [PRNigeria](https://prnigeria.com/2015/05/28/efcc-arraigns-ex-nlng-cooperative-chief-four-others-for-n207m-fraud/)). The NNPC Lagos case shows that regulator intervention is double-edged: the Lagos Ministry dissolved the elected committee and imposed a caretaker "without exhausting internal conflict resolution"; members allege the caretaker changed bank signatories, authorised payment for a failed project and was involved in a disputed ₦180 million transaction, while the last legitimate election was December 2022 and three suits are pending ([SaharaReporters](https://saharareporters.com/2025/04/17/nnpc-cooperative-members-accuse-lagos-ministry-enabling-misappropriation-savings); [Leadership](https://leadership.ng/nnpc-retirees-lament-mismanagement-of-staff-cooperative-contributions/)); only the aggrieved members' side is on record. And the Police case shows the liquidity mechanics of property concentration: a society that paid voluntary withdrawals within 24 hours began taking two to twelve months once funds were tied up in Ikeja construction, and its response was a member platform launched in November 2022 to give "quick access to their funds" ([TheFact](https://thefact.ng/police-digitalizes-cooperative-society-operations/)). The PPMC Port Harcourt branch's own AGM report lists the pressures a committee faces from inside — low individual savings, heavy withdrawal demand, "member pressure to breach loan rules", failure to repay up-front deductions, and servicing a ₦1 billion bank loan taken by a previous committee ([PPMC PH AGM Report](https://www.ppmcphcoop.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/AGM-Report-compressed.pdf)) — a candid inventory that most societies never publish.

## Ten controls follow from the failures

The successes and failures imply a specific control set, and each control has a documented failure it answers. The first five concern money leaving the society. Land payments should clear only after independent title verification at the state lands registry and through an escrow or solicitor's account released against registered conveyance — the NLNG lesson, where ₦650 million left in two tranches to an intermediary for land the vendor did not own; no Nigerian cooperative source describes an escrow in use, which is itself a finding. Property and any single illiquid asset class should be capped as a share of total assets, with a liquidity reserve sized to exit scenarios (redeployment, divestment, retirement cohorts) rather than to historical withdrawals — the Police, NNPC and PPMC lesson. Related-party and vendor payments above a threshold should require general-meeting approval and two-of-three trustee signatures, as ASUU-Ojo's ₦100,000/10%-of-assets rule already does ([ASUU Ojo](https://asuuojocoop.com/downloads/bye-laws.pdf)). Any non-core venture — a microfinance bank, a hotel, a bank stake — should pass a formal investment-committee appraisal with a hurdle above the Treasury-bill rate, which is Mwalimu's "we didn't calculate properly" lesson ([Business Daily](https://www.businessdailyafrica.com/bd/data-hub/teachers-learn-sh10bn-loss-lesson-in-spire-deal--4108192)). And the Act's s.34 reserve should be treated as a real buffer held in cash, term deposits and treasury bills (the ASUU-Ojo rule), not as a book entry backed by plots.

The next three concern governance continuity. An annual AGM with audited accounts filed to the registrar is the single observable that separated every builder from NNPC Lagos; the NFIU calendar (accounts by 31 March, filed by 31 July, AGM thereafter) is a usable template. Two-year committee terms with a two-consecutive-term cap plus an independent inspection body (NLNG's Council of Inspectors, PPMC's Council of Scrutiny, NFIU's five-member Internal Audit Committee meeting quarterly) and registrar-witnessed elections prevent the officer-overstay the FCT recertification drive was aimed at ([NLNG Coop](https://nlng.coop.ng/About); [Abuja Digest](http://abujadigest.info/2022/06/why-all-cooperative-societies-in-fct-must-be-recertified-secretary/)). And digital member ledgers with direct-to-bank disbursement and payout (NSC, PPMC's e-applications, the Police platform) remove the record-keeping failure that left a 35-year retiree unable to prove pre-2019 contributions.

The last two concern the payroll link. Remittance should be reconciled monthly against the deduction file with an escalation ladder — written demand, Director referral, NICN claim with interest — and societies whose paymaster is a state treasury or IPPIS should recognise that counterparty as a credit exposure, provision for it, and keep a liquidity line (CFAN wholesale finance or a bank facility) sized to several months of non-remittance; *Oredo* shows the claim is winnable but takes years. And the society should run its own aggregate-deduction test for each borrower, because nobody else does: the Labour Act's one-third cap is unenforced across cooperative, microfinance and fintech lenders, Kano's three-month suspension is the only documented corrective, and the planned CoopCheck bureau does not yet exist ([BusinessDay](https://businessday.ng/news/article/21000-kano-civil-servants-indebted-to-private-loan-firms/)). For an employer, the corresponding controls are a written deduction-and-remittance agreement with a fixed remittance date, payslip itemisation, a headroom check before accepting any new third-party deduction, and a governance clause that lets the employer require audited accounts as a condition of continuing the check-off — the one lever an employer has over a society it does not own.

## Conclusion

The staff cooperative is best understood as a privately run payroll bank with a statutory charter and no prudential supervisor. Its credit risk is close to zero and its governance risk is close to total, which inverts the usual ordering a lender's analyst applies: the right due-diligence questions are not about loan quality but about who signs cheques, when the last audited AGM was held, what share of assets is land, and whether the paymaster is a state treasury. The real return on the savings leg is negative for most members in the current inflation regime, so the product's value is the embedded borrowing option and the housing lottery, and a society that cannot demonstrate reliable loan access and periodic allocation is offering members a poor trade. The same logic explains why the oil-sector societies, with 1,300–3,000 members and tens of billions in assets, look like Kenyan SACCOs while civil-service societies look like exposed creditors of their own governments.

Two shifts are under way that will change the analysis within the next few years. The federal registry, verification numbers and CoopCheck bureau will, if delivered, create the first dataset capable of answering how many staff cooperatives exist and what they hold, and will make the one-third deduction cap enforceable for the first time; until then every national figure, including the ₦1.7 trillion, should be treated as directional. And the Cooperative Bank of Nigeria will ask the strongest societies to put ₦21–50 million each into a second-tier institution — the Rwandan sequence without Rwanda's prior automation and consolidation, and with Mwalimu's Spire Bank as the cautionary precedent. For a business analyst advising an employer or a committee, the practical stance is to treat the cooperative as a regulated-in-name, self-regulated-in-fact financial institution, to import the controls above by bye-law amendment registered with the Director, and to measure what the sector has never measured: members versus non-members on payroll data.
